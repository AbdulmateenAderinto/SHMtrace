## Full original Results paragraphs for relocation review

### Results block 1

SHMtrace starts from the files that a sequencing provider or core facility returns (Figures 1 and S1). Nanopore and Illumina reads are first filtered by length and expected error[@edgar2015] and aligned with minimap2^{29} against the target together with competing references. Reads that align better to a paralog, are chimeric, or carry ten or more paralog-specific bases are removed (Figure 1B), and the same number of usable reads is then drawn at random from every library. Sanger chromatograms are assigned to animals through a sample sheet and trimmed to their high-quality core, and reads tagged with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) are collapsed to one consensus per molecule.

### Results block 2

The central step is the variant test (Figures 1A and 1C). For each substitution type, strand, and base quality, SHMtrace measures how often the AID-null libraries of the same run show that change, and asks whether a candidate is supported by more reads, at the qualities observed, than this background explains. Because quality scores overstate certainty at the highest qualities (Figure S2), the control rates are multiplied by a margin chosen by leaving one control out at a time (STAR Methods). The held-out controls then serve as decoys, in an approach inspired by target–decoy analysis[@elias2007]: SHMtrace keeps the most permissive cutoff at which estimated false calls are at most 5% of calls. This is an empirical estimate targeting 5%, not a guarantee: it relies on a few controls representing the sample's background, and the same controls also choose the margin. Candidates arise only where a read already differs, so the set tested is data-dependent, and its null p-values need not be superuniform, as Benjamini–Hochberg[@benjamini1995] requires; SHMtrace counts false calls in controls selected the same way. Alternative alleles carried by at least half the reads at a position are set aside as likely germline or parental differences. This is a heuristic, not a test: a mutation shared by a lineage that dominates a library crosses the same threshold, so every site it removes is reported with its read count and frequency.

### Results block 4

Two C-to-T candidates in an external wild-type Sμ library illustrate how matched controls distinguish candidate mutations from recurrent background (WT3; Figure 2). The substitution at position 165, within a WRC hotspot (TACCT), was supported by 20 of 2,000 reads, compared with 15 of 1,941 reads at position 97 (AGCTT). Both had support on both strands and similar median base qualities (Q34 and Q33, respectively). However, no control supported the position-165 substitution at Q≥20, whereas KO1 carried the position-97 substitution in 14 of 1,879 reads at a median quality of Q33. SHMtrace retained the position-165 candidate (p = 5 × 10^{−19}) and rejected the recurrent position-97 candidate (p = 1), which the Geneious rule called in two controls and all three wild-type libraries. Across WT3, 743 distinct substitutions were evaluated as potential mutations, of which 88 met the control-calibrated calling threshold (p ≤ 0.009; Figure 2D). The remaining candidates could not be distinguished confidently from background.

### Results block 5

SHMtrace compares groups using one summary value per biological replicate, with Welch tests on log-transformed rates, negative-binomial tests with parametric bootstrapping, and permutation tests (Figure 1D; Figure S3 shows each output with an example). It characterizes mutation patterns through substitution spectra, positional clustering, hotspot use and an S5F-based targeting index[@yaari2013]. Additional analyses estimate single-read mutations that could not be called individually, group reads into clones by their junction sequences, reconstruct read-level mutation trees and estimate statistical power. The command-line and desktop interfaces run the same analysis engine.

### Results block 7

We planted 60 AID-weighted substitutions into each held-out control library, preserving base qualities and assigning 1–20 supporting reads per site, with three repetitions per control (Figure 3). On J_{H}4, SHMtrace recalled 81% overall, and 95% of mutations were supported by at least three reads, with 2.6 false calls per library (observed FDR 0.050). In contrast, Geneious recalled more single-read mutations (60% versus 31%) but produced 4.9 false calls per library (FDR 0.083). Default LoFreq recalled none; disabling its short-read-oriented base-alignment quality correction yielded 34% recall, restricted to mutations with at least five supporting reads. Permissive settings increased recall to 56% with 4.4 false calls per library (Figures 3A and 3D).

### Results block 8

ClairS[@zheng2026] and DeepSomatic[@park2026] were supplied with the other AID-null libraries as matched normals. Neither recovered planted mutations at default settings. Across 2,160 substitutions on J_{H}4, N114 V_{H}, Sμ and *Pim1*, permissive ClairS recovered none; permissive DeepSomatic recalled 19%, 18%, 5% and 3%, respectively, with at most 0.4 false calls per library. Recall increased at higher allele fractions, approaching complete recovery at 20% on J_{H}4 (Figure 3C). On real J_{H}4 libraries, ClairS reported one mutation and DeepSomatic at most 13 across three mice, compared with 221, 344 and 73 SHMtrace calls. Corresponding N114 V_{H} totals were one and at most 14, compared with 70, 49 and 173. ClairS rejected both Wt3 candidates supported by at least 20% of reads. Most SHMtrace-retained calls had much lower fractions: among 1,197 calls across four loci, 53% occurred in <1% of reads, 93% in <5% and 99.5% in <20% (Figure 2E). Germline callers Clair3 and DeepVariant recovered none of the 2,160 planted substitutions, consistent with their intended operating range (Table S1).

### Results block 9

On N114 V_{H}, SHMtrace recalled 90% of planted substitutions, including 58% supported by one read, with 2.6 false calls per library (FDR 0.045; Figure 3B). Geneious recalled 88% with 2.8 false calls (FDR 0.050); LoFreq without base-alignment quality recalled 34%. On external Sμ and *Pim1* controls at 2,000 reads, SHMtrace recalled 63% and 72% at FDR 0.055 and 0.035. Geneious recalled 59% and 64% at FDR 0.114 and 0.050; LoFreq recalled 0–26%, with permissive FDR 0.27–0.52 (Figure 3D).

### Results block 16

At 500 reads per library, read-weighted mutation frequencies were within 2% of the simulated values for both J_{H}4 and N114 V_{H} (Figure 3G). Unique-variant density was less consistently recovered: J_{H}4 estimates were 97% of the true density at 500 reads and 94% at 1,000 reads, whereas N114 V_{H} reached 77% at 1,000 reads. This endpoint counts each distinct substitution once and divides by the number of analyzed bases. As sequencing depth increases, additional reads can carry mutations already counted, increasing the denominator without adding new variants; depth also affects detection of rare mutations. Including the aggregate estimate of single-read mutations that were not called individually improved recovery to 96–100% of the true density across the simulated scenarios. In a separate test, planting 25, 50 or 100 single-read mutations increased this estimate by an average of 95% of the number introduced (Figure S8A). Comparisons of unique-variant density therefore require matched sequencing depth.

### Results block 19

We next compared callers on real libraries with matched AID-null controls (Table S2; Figure 4). These included our J_{H}4 intron libraries from germinal-center B cells of three wild-type (Wt) and three *Aicda*^{−/−} mice, N114 hybridoma subclones transduced with AID or empty vector, and three loci from an independent laboratory's public nanopore dataset: the Sμ switch region in LPS-activated B cells and in CH12 cells, and the AID off-target gene *Pim1*. Every caller analysed exactly the same reads (Table S3).

### Results block 20

SHMtrace retained 1–4 calls across the three controls per assay, versus 5–20 for Geneious (Figures 4A–4E and 4G). Geometric-mean unique-density separation, with zero-call controls assigned one-event values, was 141-fold for J_{H}4, 85-fold for N114 V_{H} (Figure S9), 66-fold for primary-cell Sμ and 94-fold for CH12 Sμ, compared with 16–62-fold for Geneious and 2–40-fold for LoFreq configurations (Figure 4F). J_{H}4 Wt counts were 221, 344 and 73 versus 0, 2 and 0 in controls; Geneious yielded 203, 337 and 73 versus 2, 6 and 5. Default LoFreq called 0, 11 and 0 Wt variants. Disabling base-alignment quality yielded 22 in Wt2 versus 221 for SHMtrace. At 500 reads, J_{H}4 and N114 V_{H} had reached 90% and 59% of calls observed at the largest tested depth (Figure S10), without establishing saturation. External Sμ Wt counts were 65–157 for SHMtrace and 52–131 for Geneious, with 0–2 versus 2–10 per control. Control mismatches were position-dependent (Figures S11A and S11B). After germline exclusion, representative Wt and null J_{H}4 libraries contained 34 and 35 other high-quality mismatches, while the Wt library added 5,643 at called sites, often shared within clones (Figures S11C and S11D).

### Results block 23

The two CH12 AID off-target loci, off-target 1 and off-target 2, tested a genetic perturbation expected to reduce AID targeting[@seija2026]. Off-target 1 Wt libraries carried 69, 50, and 55 calls versus 0, 2, and 0 in *Aicda*^{−/−} controls, with densities of 3.07 versus 0.04 per 10^{5} bases (Welch p = 9.7 × 10^{−4} on log density; 0.024 on frequency). Off-target 2 counts were 42, 28, and 23 versus 2, 0, and 0, with densities of 1.68 versus 0.04 (p = 5.5 × 10^{−4} and 0.030). G:C substitutions accounted for 97% of 174 off-target 1 calls and 97% of 93 off-target 2 calls. MLLT1/MLLT3 double knockout reduced counts to 2, 0, and 2 at off-target 1 (0.07 per 10^{5} bases) and 1, 0, and 0 at off-target 2 (0.02), statistically indistinguishable from *Aicda*^{−/−} controls (p = 0.51 and 0.43). No fold is reported because a zero-count convention would determine its magnitude.

### Results block 24

SHMtrace multiplies the control-derived error rates by an empirically selected calibration factor to account for background that the fitted model underestimates. This adjustment increases the expected error probability used in the test, so a candidate requires stronger evidence to be called; it does not change the observed mutation count. The factor is selected by testing each control against the remaining controls and choosing the smallest tested multiplier for which no more than 1% of held-out candidate tests yield p < 0.01. Without adjustment, 5.4% of J_{H}4 and 9.6% of N114 V_{H} held-out tests fell below this threshold. Factors of five for the shared J_{H}4 window and ten for full-length J_{H}4 and N114 V_{H} reduced these proportions to 1.0%, 0.7% and 0.5%, respectively (Figure 4H). These factors are empirical calibration adjustments: they do not imply that sequencing errors are universally five or ten times higher than the fitted model, and they do not by themselves deliver a 5% false-discovery rate, which the decoy threshold below addresses separately.

### Results block 25

Read preparation excluded 44–79% of delivered J_{H}4 reads per library, or 67% overall. Expected-error filtering and competitive alignment accounted for 29% and 25% of delivered reads, respectively (Figure 4I). Competitive alignment alone did not remove all paralog-derived reads: full-length paralog reads could align preferentially to the longer target reference when the competing paralog reference was shorter. Without an additional filter based on paralog-specific bases, these reads generated 35–54 false variant calls per simulated library.

### Results block 27

SHMtrace processed 182 Sanger chromatograms from the six nanopore-sequenced mice, retaining 134 clones after alignment and sequence-quality filtering for comparison across the shared 529-bp window (Figures 5 and S13). SHMtrace-derived mutation profiles distinguished Wt from *Aicda*^{−/−} mice on both platforms: Sanger showed 38-fold separation (p = 8.8 × 10^{−4}), and nanopore showed 141-fold separation when zero-call controls were assigned one event (p = 0.0027; Welch tests on log densities, n = 3 mice per group). Pooled substitution spectra were similar across the twelve substitution types (descriptive Pearson r = 0.92; slope = 0.97), as were positional profiles in 25-bp bins (r = 0.94). Among G:C mutations, 58% of Sanger and 54% of nanopore calls occurred at WRC/GYW hotspots, which accounted for 20% of eligible sites in the window. Among A:T mutations, 70% and 69%, respectively, occurred at WA/TW motifs, compared with 50% of eligible sites. No platform-by-substitution-type interaction was detected (p = 0.62), although this does not establish equivalence with three mice per group. Absolute unique-variant densities are not directly comparable between workflows because the Sanger analysis counts mutations in individual cloned products, whereas the nanopore analysis counts distinct variants among 500 reads.

### Results block 29

A published J_{H}4 study compared four Wt and four *Maea*^{fl/fl}*Mb1*^{cre} mice with *Aicda*^{−/−} controls using SHMTool[@maccarthy2009,@barbulescu2024]. After reconstruction of clone assignments, SHMtrace processed 293 chromatograms in 16 s; while curation took substantially longer (Figure S14; STAR Methods). Mutation frequency was 4.6-fold lower in *Maea*-deficient mice versus 4.4-fold in the publication (one-tailed t tests, p = 2.3 × 10^{−4} and 0.0143). G:C transitions fell from 19% to 11% (published, 19% to 11%; p = 2.9 × 10^{−4} and 0.0028), and transversions rose from 15% to 21% (14% to 20%; p = 0.025 and 0.0285). Spectra correlated at r = 0.99; mouse rankings at ρ = 0.95. Under the published clone-count × 577-bp convention, frequencies averaged 1.0-fold the published values. Controls carried two mutations in 19 clones (p = 3.0 × 10^{−4} and 0.0039 versus Wt and knockout). Four traces assigned to two mice and 134 identical mutated-clone pairs were retained as filed and flagged.

### Results block 30

Reanalysis of 402 *Mkln1*-study chromatograms[@barbulescu2024] found an 8.0-fold frequency reduction versus the published 7.8-fold (p = 0.0038 and 0.014). G:C transversions increased from 15% to 29% versus 16% to 27% in the published tables (p = 0.0078 and 0.0040). ### Results block 31

Both of those studies come from the laboratory that produced the libraries analysed here, so we also re-analysed a Sanger study from an unrelated laboratory, at a different locus: Thientosapol et al. hypermutated a SW_{HEL} V_{H}10 knock-in V region in germinal-centre B cells and reported that inactivating *Samhd1* raises transitions at C:G base pairs and lowers transversions at A:T and at C:G in both strands[@thientosapol2018]. Its 1,795 deposited clones were analysed from their own records alone, with the germline taken as their majority consensus (STAR Methods); the 558-bp window this yields matches the 560 bp the study analyzed. Across the five arms and the twelve substitution types, SHMtrace counted 2,804 of the 2,809 mutations the study tabulates (Pearson r = 1.0000 over the 60 cells; Figure S16), and mutations per sequence agreed to two decimal places in every arm. The four substitution classes the study tested moved as reported: transversions fell at A:T (p = 9.7 × 10^{−11}) and at C:G (p = 7.7 × 10^{−6}), transitions at C:G rose (p = 1.2 × 10^{−10}), and transitions at A:T did not move (p = 0.14). For total mutation the study reported a decrease that did not reach significance (p = 0.07, one-way ANOVA); a Welch test on per-animal log frequency, a different test, found it lower (p = 3.8 × 10^{−4}), so that comparison agrees in direction but not in inference. The deposit names 12 wild-type hosts where the study reports 13, one of them carrying about twice the clones of any other. That host is retained, because nothing in the records says which clone came from which animal; every animal-level test was repeated without it and none changed (STAR Methods). The *Msh2*^{−/−} arm, which SHMtrace was not told was a repair mutant, showed the expected collapse of A:T mutations, from 56% to 5% of all mutations. Because this study deposits sequences rather than chromatograms, it tests the calling, the spectrum and the group statistics but not the trace reading.

### Results block 39

HMCES-deficient Ramos clones had 3.8-fold more deletions and 2.5-fold more insertions than Wt clones (deletions: p = 1.0 × 10^{−4}; n = 6 per group)[@wu2022] (Figure 7D). Re-analysis of the deposit, which the original study does not report, found substitution calling sensitive to one of its three AID-knockout controls, which carried 36 substitutions, 61% at G:C, while the other two had none. Including this library among the decoys used to select the calling threshold prevented any substitutions from being called in the Wt or HMCES-deficient clones. SHMtrace’s noisy-control rule therefore excluded it from threshold selection, while retaining it in background-model fitting and documenting the exclusion. With this adjustment, Wt and HMCES-deficient clones yielded 52–99 substitution calls each, with 86–97% at G:C. In a sensitivity analysis, treating the noisy library as a sample rather than a control yielded 277–413 calls per Wt or HMCES-deficient clone, with 71–84% at G:C. The difference shows that control assignment materially affects the inferred substitution burden. The same exclusion rule was triggered in the mOrange analysis.

### Results block 41

A published mOrange reporter study compared AID-ER with catalytically inactive AID-E58Q-ER in NIH-3T3 cells carrying an empty vector, Ugi (Uracil-DNA glycosylase inhibitor) or UNG (Uracil DNA glycosylase), using 36-bp sequencing reads[@perezduran2012]. Counting all mismatches at Q>20 yielded background frequencies of 0.31–0.72 per 10^{3} bases in inactive-AID libraries, with only two-fold separation between active and inactive AID in the empty-vector condition. SHMtrace used the inactive-AID libraries from this condition to calibrate substitution calling across all three repair conditions. It retained 115 substitutions across nine active-AID libraries, all at G:C bases, with 75–100% at WRC/GYW hotspots. Inactive-AID libraries carrying Ugi or UNG retained zero and two calls, respectively. Separation between active and inactive AID increased to 20-fold with empty vector, 1,002-fold with Ugi and 103-fold with UNG; the Ugi comparison assigned the zero-call controls one event. Among retained substitutions, the transition share decreased from 95% with Ugi to 4% with UNG, whereas uncorrected inactive-AID backgrounds contained 43–66% transitions (Figure 7E). The mean paired difference between Ugi and UNG was 92 percentage points (95% CI 74–110; p = 0.0021), although the small number of calls per library limits precision.

### Results block 45

PacBio Sμ–Sγ1 junctions[@vincendeau2022] read the donor, the join and the acceptor on one 6,126-bp molecule, so the junction module was applied to a class-switch phenotype it was not tuned for (Figure S20). Mean microhomology was 1.01 nt in wild type and unchanged in *Shld1*^{−/−} (1.02 nt; p = 0.92), but rose without XLF (1.69 nt; p = 0.0092) and in the double mutant (1.59 nt; p = 9.3 × 10^{−5}), where junctions with ≥4 nt of microhomology rose from 7% to 18% (p = 0.0053) and blunt junctions fell from 21% to 7% (p = 0.0028); *Shld1*^{−/−} junctions instead carried more inserted bases (48% versus 31%; p = 0.0074). This is the shift toward microhomology-mediated end joining expected when classical non-homologous end joining is impaired, with no parameter fitted to the dataset. Reads per unique junction vary several-fold between samples (1.1–23.3), so junction counts measure long-range PCR recovery rather than switching efficiency; junction shape, not calibrated calls, is compared, and re-aligning each acceptor segment on its own places 19–47% of unique junctions at more than one equally good position, so breakpoints within repeats remain approximate.
