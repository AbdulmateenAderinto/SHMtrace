## Results

### SHMtrace calibrates substitutions against AID-null controls

SHMtrace starts from delivered reads or Sanger chromatograms (Figures 1 and S1). Read libraries undergo length and expected-error filtering[@edgar2015], competitive alignment with minimap2[@li2018], paralog checks and matched-depth sampling. Sanger traces are assigned through a sample sheet and quality-trimmed; UMI-tagged reads can be collapsed to molecular consensuses (STAR Methods).

For each substitution, SHMtrace tests read support against control error rates stratified by substitution type, strand and quality, incorporating position-specific background (Figures 1A and 1C). A leave-one-control-out margin adjusts underestimated error (Figure S2); held-out controls then set an empirical target–decoy threshold targeting 5% false discoveries[@elias2007]. This estimate depends on representative controls and is not a general FDR guarantee. Data-dependent candidate selection motivates empirical calibration rather than assuming valid Benjamini–Hochberg p-values[@benjamini1995]. Alleles in ≥50% of reads are excluded as likely germline or parental differences; genuine dominant-lineage mutations can also cross this heuristic threshold, and exclusions are reported.

The read-library test can retain a candidate supported by a single read; repeated sequencing of the same original molecule is not required. SHMtrace uses a separate Sanger counting route (Figure 1C): every substitution at base quality ≥ 20 is counted, and the AID-null clones' mismatch rate gives the expected background count, reported separately without subtraction. The germline filter, window, automatic checks and animal-level comparisons are shared. The Sanger results below therefore test trace reading, counting, bookkeeping and group comparisons, not the control-calibrated test.

Two C-to-T candidates in Sμ WT3 had similar qualities and support on both strands (Figure 2). Position 165, in a WRC hotspot, lacked Q≥20 control support and was retained (p = 5 × 10^{−19}); position 97 recurred in controls and was rejected (p = 1). Of 743 candidates, 88 met the threshold (p ≤ 0.009); the remainder were unresolved against background.

Group comparisons use one value per biological replicate, with log-rate Welch, bootstrapped negative-binomial and permutation tests (Figure 1D). Outputs include spectra, hotspot use, S5F targeting[@yaari2013], clustering, aggregate single-read estimates, junction-defined clones, read-level trees and power estimates (Figure S3). Desktop and command-line interfaces share the analysis engine.

### Planted mutations and simulated lineages quantify recall and false calls

We planted 60 AID-weighted substitutions into each held-out control library, preserving base qualities and assigning 1–20 supporting reads per site, with three repetitions per control (Figure 3). On J_{H}4, SHMtrace recalled 81% overall and 95% among events supported by at least three reads, with 2.6 false calls per library (observed FDR 0.050). In contrast, Geneious recalled more single-read mutations (60% versus 31%) but produced 4.9 false calls per library (FDR 0.083). Default LoFreq recalled none; disabling its short-read-oriented base-alignment quality correction yielded 34% recall, restricted to mutations with at least five supporting reads. Permissive settings increased recall to 56% with 4.4 false calls per library (Figures 3A and 3D).

ClairS[@zheng2026] and DeepSomatic[@park2026] received peer controls as matched normals. Neither recovered planted events at defaults. Across 2,160 substitutions, permissive ClairS recovered none; DeepSomatic recalled 19%, 18%, 5% and 3% on J_{H}4, N114 V_{H}, Sμ and *Pim1*, respectively. Recall improved at higher allele fractions (Figure 3C), whereas 53% of 1,197 retained SHMtrace calls occurred in <1% of reads (Figure 2E). Germline callers Clair3 and DeepVariant recovered none, consistent with their intended use (Table S1).

On N114 V_{H}, SHMtrace recalled 90% of planted substitutions, including 58% supported by one read, with 2.6 false calls per library (FDR 0.045; Figure 3B). Geneious recalled 88% with 2.8 false calls (FDR 0.050); LoFreq without base-alignment quality recalled 34%. On external Sμ and *Pim1* controls at 2,000 reads, SHMtrace recalled 63% and 72% at FDR 0.055 and 0.035. Geneious recalled 59% and 64% at FDR 0.114 and 0.050; LoFreq recalled 0–26%, with permissive FDR 0.27–0.52 (Figure 3D).

Calling the same libraries at targets from 1% to 30% tested whether the threshold delivers the rate it targets. At the default 5%, the pooled false-discovery proportion was 3.5–5.5% across the four loci, and looser targets stayed at or below their stated rate except J_{H}4 at 10% (13.8%) (Figure S4A). Targets below 5% did not deliver their stated rate on J_{H}4, Sμ or *Pim1* (2.0–6.1% at a 1% target; N114 V_{H}, 0.5%): false calls per library levelled off while recall fell, from 81% to 17% on J_{H}4 (Figures S4B and S4C).

Varying the controls through the whole procedure on J_{H}4 showed how many are needed (Figure S5). One control leaves no decoys, so the threshold falls back to Benjamini–Hochberg: it recalled 68% of planted lineage mutations, against 93% with two controls and 95% with three or four, at false-discovery proportions of 2.8–3.1%. All-null libraries yielded no calls with any number of controls (40 runs). One control carrying recurrent artifacts changed false calls little, with or without the noisy-control rule. On Sμ, AID-null controls from a second experiment performed like the matched controls (66–68% versus 63% recall), and pooling all five lowered false calls from 2.2 to 0.8 per library.

Diluting real hypermutated reads into AID-null reads tested the estimates on mutations AID made, with their clonality and clustering (Figure S6). On N114 V_{H}, mutation frequency was 0.87–0.97 of the expected value at 25–50% donor reads, and calls were made from 1–5% donor reads. On J_{H}4, detection was all-or-nothing and depended on the donor: calls began at 2% donor reads for Wt3 and at 50% for Wt2, and no call was made below that.

Indel validation used ten planted insertions or deletions of 1–40 bp per library, supported by 1–20 reads, with eight draws per control and error models fitted to the other controls. Among events supported by at least three reads, recall was 72% on J_{H}4 and 91% on N114 V_{H}, with 0.33 and zero false calls per library. Events supported by one or two reads were excluded by the three-read requirement. J_{H}4 single-base homopolymer deletions were least reliably recovered (19%); recall was 100% for 2-bp insertions and 89% for deletions ≥10 bp (Figure S7).

We next simulated approximately 40 B-cell clones per J_{H}4 library, with clone sizes following a power-law distribution. Mutations were introduced at positions sampled according to S5F mutability, including mutations shared across an entire clone, restricted to individual branches, or unique to individual sequences (Figure 3E). SHMtrace recalled 83% of single-read mutations versus 57% for Geneious, with FDR 0.037 versus 0.012 and 13.7 versus 4.0 false calls per library. In null libraries, false calls averaged 0.7 versus 4.3. Junction clustering recovered clone assignments with adjusted Rand indices[@hubert1985] of 0.94–0.98. When an unmutated clone contributed 60% of reads, read-weighted frequency was 6.2 versus a true 6.0 per 10^{3} bp, while the majority-rule clone-weighted measure recovered 94% of its corresponding true burden (14.6 versus 15.5; Figure 3F).

The dominant unmutated clone could not trigger the ≥50% allele filter. Repeating the simulation with a mutated dominant clone showed no loss at 40% abundance, but all mutations crossing the threshold at 60% or 80% were removed. Read-weighted and clone-weighted estimates fell by as much as 62% and 34%, respectively (STAR Methods). Excluded sites are reported for inspection.

Read-weighted frequencies were within 2% of simulated truth at 500 reads (Figure 3G). Unique-variant density varied with depth because repeated variants increase exposure without adding discoveries, while rare-event detection also changes. Adding the aggregate single-read estimate recovered 96–100% of truth; independently planted single-read events were recovered at 95% in aggregate (Figure S8A). Unique-density comparisons require matched depth.

Group-test calibration was assessed in 300 simulations per condition, with between-animal coefficient of variation 1 (Figure 3H). Under no true difference, pooled-read tests returned p < 0.05 in 91% of 3-versus-3 and 94% of 5-versus-5 experiments. Negative-binomial rejection rates were 5.0% and 5.7%. With three animals per group, that test detected two-fold and four-fold changes in 12% and 30% of simulations, illustrating the limited power at this sample size.

### Matched controls set the background in real libraries

We next compared callers on real libraries with matched AID-null controls (Table S2; Figure 4). These included our J_{H}4 intron libraries from germinal-center B cells of three wild-type (Wt) and three *Aicda*^{−/−} mice, N114 hybridoma subclones transduced with AID or empty vector, and two loci in three assay contexts from an independent laboratory's public nanopore dataset: the Sμ switch region in LPS-activated B cells and in CH12 cells, and the AID off-target gene *Pim1*. Every caller analysed exactly the same reads (Table S3).

SHMtrace retained 1–4 calls across three controls per assay versus 5–20 for Geneious (Figures 4A–4E and 4G). Geometric-mean unique-density separation was 141-, 85-, 66- and 94-fold for J_{H}4, N114 V_{H}, primary-cell Sμ and CH12 Sμ, respectively, using one-event values for zero-call controls (Figure 4F). At 500 reads, J_{H}4 and N114 V_{H} reached 90% and 59% of discoveries at the largest tested depth, without establishing saturation (Figures S9 and S10). Background mismatches were position-dependent and separable from abundant clone-shared calls (Figure S11).

The same N114 V_{H} reads were analyzed by IgBLAST[@ye2013] and SHazaM[@gupta2015], which count every mismatch to the germline (Figure S12). With the IMGT germline the AID libraries were 2-fold above the empty-vector libraries, because the parental line differs from it (99.3% identity) and sequencing errors count as mutations. Using the parental V as germline raised this to 6-fold, and masking bases below Q20 to 32-fold, compared with 188-fold for SHMtrace.

At *Pim1*, mutation calls in AID-competent libraries remained close to the AID-null background. At 2,000 reads per library, SHMtrace yielded 1.8-fold separation between groups, with four calls across the controls, compared with 1.6-fold separation and eleven control calls for Geneious. Increasing depth to all available reads (3,238–3,447 per library) did not clearly resolve the signal: SHMtrace detected 3–10 variants per AID-competent library and 0–3 per AID-null library, while Geneious detected 5–7 and 0–4, respectively (Table S1).

The two CH12 AID off-target loci, off-target 1 and off-target 2, tested a genetic perturbation expected to reduce AID targeting[@seija2026]. Off-target 1 Wt libraries carried 69, 50, and 55 calls versus 0, 2, and 0 in *Aicda*^{−/−} controls, with densities of 3.07 versus 0.04 per 10^{5} bases (Welch p = 9.7 × 10^{−4} on log density; 0.024 on frequency). Off-target 2 counts were 42, 28, and 23 versus 2, 0, and 0, with densities of 1.68 versus 0.04 (p = 5.5 × 10^{−4} and 0.030). G:C substitutions accounted for 97% of 174 off-target 1 calls and 97% of 93 off-target 2 calls. MLLT1/MLLT3 double knockout reduced counts to 2, 0, and 2 at off-target 1 (0.07 per 10^{5} bases) and 1, 0, and 0 at off-target 2 (0.02), with no detected difference from *Aicda*^{−/−} controls (p = 0.51 and 0.43). No fold is reported because a zero-count convention would determine its magnitude.

Leave-one-control-out error margins of five for the shared J_{H}4 window and ten for full-length J_{H}4 and N114 V_{H} reduced held-out candidate tests with p<0.01 to 1.0%, 0.7% and 0.5%, respectively (Figure 4H). This calibrates the error model; the separate decoy threshold estimates the false-discovery proportion.

Read preparation removed 67% of delivered J_{H}4 reads overall (Figure 4I). Competitive alignment required an additional paralog-base filter: without it, simulated paralog reads produced 35–54 false variants per library (STAR Methods).

### SHMtrace-generated nanopore and Sanger profiles agree in matched mice

SHMtrace processed 182 Sanger chromatograms from the six nanopore-sequenced mice, retaining 134 clones after alignment and sequence-quality filtering for comparison across the shared 529-bp window (Figures 5 and S13). SHMtrace-derived mutation profiles distinguished Wt from *Aicda*^{−/−} mice on both platforms: Sanger showed 38-fold separation (p = 8.8 × 10^{−4}), and nanopore showed 141-fold separation when zero-call controls were assigned one event (p = 0.0027; Welch tests on log densities, n = 3 mice per group). Pooled substitution spectra were similar across the twelve substitution types (descriptive Pearson r = 0.92; slope = 0.97), as were positional profiles in 25-bp bins (r = 0.94). Among G:C mutations, 58% of Sanger and 54% of nanopore calls occurred at WRC/GYW hotspots, which accounted for 20% of eligible sites in the window. Among A:T mutations, 70% and 69%, respectively, occurred at WA/TW motifs, compared with 50% of eligible sites. No platform-by-substitution-type interaction was detected (p = 0.62), although this does not establish equivalence with three mice per group. Absolute unique-variant densities are not directly comparable between workflows because the Sanger analysis counts mutations in individual cloned products, whereas the nanopore analysis counts distinct variants among 500 reads.

### Sanger reanalysis recovers published comparisons and identifies discrepancies

Reanalysis of published *Maea* J_{H}4 chromatograms[@maccarthy2009,@barbulescu2024] recovered a 4.6-fold frequency reduction versus 4.4-fold published (one-tailed p = 2.3 × 10^{−4} and 0.0143; Figure S14). Spectra correlated at r = 0.99, but denominator-harmonized frequencies averaged 1.0-fold published values. Shared trace assignments and identical mutated-clone pairs were retained as filed and flagged; detailed discrepancies are in Table S4 and STAR Methods.

Reanalysis of 402 *Mkln1*-study chromatograms[@barbulescu2025] found an 8.0-fold frequency reduction versus the published 7.8-fold (p = 0.0038 and 0.014). G:C transversions increased from 15% to 29% versus 16% to 27% in the published tables (p = 0.0078 and 0.0040). Spectrum correlations were r = 0.97 for Wt and 0.62 for knockout (Figure S15; Table S4).

An independent laboratory's SW_{HEL} study[@thientosapol2018] provided 1,795 sequences across five arms (Figure S16). SHMtrace recovered 2,804 of 2,809 tabulated mutations (r = 1.0000 across 60 spectrum cells), matching mutations per sequence to two decimals. The four substitution-class comparisons agreed with the publication. Total mutation decreased in both analyses, but significance differed under different tests. A potentially pooled host was retained; excluding it did not change the reanalysis conclusions (STAR Methods). The *Msh2*^{−/−} arm showed the expected A:T collapse. Deposited sequences evaluate counting, spectra and group statistics, without testing chromatogram reading.

### Long reads retain whole-amplicon profiles and within-lineage linkage

Nanopore sequencing extended SHMtrace analysis of the J_{H}4 intron from the shared 529-bp Sanger window to a 1,141-bp interval spanning positions 90–1,230 (Figure 6A). Across this longer interval, Wt unique-variant density was 229-fold above controls. Approximately 69% of mutations occurred within the first 450 bp, with a seven-fold decline between the first and last 250 bp, consistent with the reported decline downstream of V(D)J[@lebecque1990]. Substitution spectra across the full interval closely matched those within the Sanger window (Pearson r = 0.989; Figure S17).

Wt mutations preferentially occurred at positions predicted to undergo SHM by the S5F model, which assigns mutability according to the surrounding five-base sequence. The targeting index, defined as mean predicted mutability at mutated positions divided by the mean across the analysis window, ranged from 1.22 to 1.36, indicating 22–36% enrichment over the window average (permutation p ≤ 0.015). Calls per position were also 4.1-fold higher in the highest-mutability quintile than in the lowest (Figure 6B). In contrast, *Aicda*^{−/−} mismatch positions showed no enrichment (index = 1.00; p = 0.50). Because S5F mutability is not used in the variant-calling test, this analysis provides a separate check that the detected mutations follow an SHM-associated targeting pattern.

Junction sequences upstream of J_{H}4 group reads into B-cell lineages (Figure 6C). In Wt2, one largely unmutated lineage accounted for 81% of junction-assigned reads. Read-weighted frequency was 2.8 per 10^{3} bp, compared with 16.3 when each lineage's majority-rule mutation profile was counted once. Wt3 and Wt4 changed less (22.5 to 24.9 and 4.9 to 5.3). The largest Wt3 lineage comprised 69 reads and four founder mutations, with five inferred sublineages extending to 17 mutations (Figure 6D). 70% of mutated reads fitted one tree; 7/1,128 mutation pairs failed the four-gamete test. Without UMIs, PCR recombination and residual errors remain possible, so these are read-level reconstructions rather than proven cellular lineages.

### Public datasets test transfer across platforms and experimental designs

Three public Illumina studies tested different biological contexts (Figure 7). At J_{H}4 positions 53–122, Wt mice showed 56-fold greater mutation frequency than AID-null mice (log-rate Welch p = 0.005; n = 7 and 4). *Ldha* deletion showed no detectable difference from Wt (Mann–Whitney p = 0.75), consistent with the original study[@sharma2023] (Figure 7A).

In *Aicda*^{−/−}*Ung*^{−/−}*Msh2*^{−/−} CH12F3 cells, ectopic AID increased frequency 40-fold over empty vector (p = 0.00781; n = 4 each), with one control call in 20,000 reads[@wang2023]. All AID calls were C→T/G→A, consistent with loss of uracil excision and mismatch repair; R50E yielded none (Figure 7B). R174E/R178E appeared more active but gave only 17% transitions, a 56% cluster within positions 164–172 and targeting index 0.73 versus 2.46 for AID. These features suggest a library-specific artifact absent from controls (Figure 7C). Establishing its source would require additional sequencing provenance or independent material.

HMCES-deficient Ramos clones[@wu2022] had 3.8-fold more deletions and 2.5-fold more insertions (deletion p = 1.0 × 10^{−4}; n=6 per group; Figure 7D). Substitution calls depended strongly on a noisy AID-null library. A rule developed after inspecting this dataset excluded it from decoy thresholding, retaining it in model fitting and reporting the exclusion. Adjusted calls were 52–99 per Wt/HMCES-deficient clone; treating that control as a sample instead yielded 277–413. This sensitivity limits substitution-burden inference (STAR Methods); the rule also acted in mOrange.

On dual-UMI R10 rRNA-operon reads[@karst2021], consensus reduced error 57-fold (95% CI 35–99) to 0.17%, with residual errors concentrated in homopolymer indels. At ≥10 reads per family, substitution error approached one per 10,000 bases (Figure S8D). Critically, this is a technical consensus benchmark rather than an SHM experiment.

In the mOrange study[@perezduran2012], inactive-AID controls calibrated active AID across empty-vector, Ugi and UNG contexts (Figure 7E). SHMtrace retained 115 substitutions, all at G:C, with 75–100% at WRC/GYW hotspots. Active/inactive separation was 20-, 1,002- and 103-fold, respectively; Ugi zeros used one-event values. Ugi-to-UNG transition shares fell from 95% to 4%. The paired mean difference was 92 percentage points (95% CI 74–110; paired t, p = 0.0021); few calls per library limit precision.

Two engineered-diversification datasets extended the evaluation to mutations measured relative to experimental starting constructs (Figure S18). In a study using a common GFP substrate[@liu2018], no-editor libraries yielded at most one call in 20,000 reads. AID substitutions were more frequently located at WRC/GYW hotspots than APOBEC3A substitutions, both in Wt cells (56% versus 14%; p = 0.0040) and in UNG/MSH2-deficient cells (37% versus 20%; p = 9.0 × 10^{−4}). In the repair-deficient background, however, their transition shares were similar (78% versus 76%; p = 0.74), showing that transition enrichment alone could not distinguish the two enzymes.

The second engineered dataset, a CRISPR-X antibody-evolution study, diversified an antibody V_{H} and published the mutations it found in each selected cluster[@devilder2019]. Comparing the two lists position by position, SHMtrace recovered 150 of the 150 published mutations and called none the authors did not, across all 42 cluster entries (Figure S18B). Because the comparator is an independently curated list rather than another run of the same software, this is the sharpest available test of variant identity on this route.

Two further datasets tested transfer to another laboratory's switch-region libraries and to a fourth platform, and showed where a deposit limits what can be asked. Sμ libraries under PI3Kδ inhibition[@compagno2017] confound genotype with library layout: six of nine wild-type libraries are single-end whereas every *Aicda*^{−/−} library is paired, and the mates do not overlap, so only the twelve paired libraries reach the switch repeats where AID acts (Figure S19). Analysed over those repeats, the nine *Aicda*^{−/−} libraries returned one call between them against 81–172 per wild-type library (p = 0.0021), and the wild-type calls carried the AID signature: targeting index 1.52–1.67 and 31–35% C→T or G→A. The calibration therefore transfers to an independent laboratory, platform and perturbation. The drug comparison does not follow from this deposit, because only one wild-type replicate per treatment reaches the repeats.

PacBio Sμ–Sγ1 junctions[@vincendeau2022] tested junction structure rather than calibrated mutation calls (Figure S20). Mean microhomology was 1.01 nt in Wt and 1.02 nt in *Shld1*^{−/−}, rising to 1.69 nt without XLF and 1.59 nt in double mutants. Double mutants showed more ≥4-nt microhomology and fewer blunt junctions; *Shld1*^{−/−} showed more insertions. These patterns support recovery of a known repair phenotype. Counts reflect PCR recovery rather than switching efficiency; 19–47% of junctions had alternative equally good acceptor placements (STAR Methods).

## Discussion

Measuring somatic hypermutation has long meant choosing between reading molecules accurately and reading enough of them. This study supports a cloning-free SHM workflow by estimating technical background from controls. SHMtrace connects delivered reads or chromatograms to mutation profiles and comparisons across biological replicates. Rather than relying on quality scores alone, it estimates the background in the AID-null libraries that SHM studies already include, at the level of substitution type, strand and base quality, and checks that calibration by holding out each control in turn. Instead of treating reads as replicates, it tests independent biological samples. Both replacements held: 81% of planted J_{H}4 mutations and 90% on N114 V_{H} were recovered at false-discovery proportions near the default target, while a quality-based caller either missed most true mutations or, made permissive, called as much background as the uncorrected rule, and pooling reads produced false positives in more than 90% of simulated experiments without a true difference. Together, these results support a practical route from deeply sequenced amplicons to interpretable SHM measurements across biological replicates.

The gap this fills is specific, and it sits between two mature bodies of software. Repertoire toolkits identify and summarise mutations well: IgBLAST assigns germlines[@ye2013], Change-O and SHazaM quantify load[@gupta2015], SHMTool compares datasets[@maccarthy2009], and S5F models targeting[@yaari2013]. They begin, however, from mismatches already taken to be real, which is why counting every mismatch to the germline separated AID from empty-vector N114 libraries by 2-fold where SHMtrace separated them by 188-fold. General-purpose variant callers do the opposite: they decide what is real, but from error models fitted to data of a different kind. LoFreq reads base qualities[@wilm2012]; ClairS and DeepSomatic were developed for tumour–normal sequencing, with different training data and calling assumptions[@zheng2026,@park2026]. The evaluated configurations did not use AID-null calibration in the manner tested here. SHMtrace closes that circuit by making the AID-null control, where available, the source of the error model, and by carrying the measurement through to the animal-level comparison that the biology requires[@lazic2010]. Its distinctive contribution is to connect control-informed mutation detection with interpretable profiles and comparisons across biological replicates in one reproducible workflow.

Seen this way, comparator performance is a matter of operating range rather than quality. LoFreq was designed for deep short-read data with calibrated qualities; its base-alignment correction suppressed nearly all nanopore calls here, and disabling it still limited recovery of low-support variants. That is not a defect on its intended data: the configurations tested here did not capture this assay's background as effectively as matched-control calibration. The measurements say why. Above Q40, control error stopped decreasing and differed across the evaluated datasets (1–9 × 10^{−5}; Figure S2), so the quality score runs out of information exactly where SHM calling needs it. ClairS and DeepSomatic recovered clean variants at higher allele fractions but few real SHM calls, and chemistry and model mismatches further qualify external-data comparisons. Clair3 and DeepVariant found none of the planted mutations, as expected of callers built to genotype a sample against a reference rather than to find a variant carried by a few reads. Geneious provided greater single-read recall on J_{H}4 and *Pim1* but more false calls on each amplicon. These are choices a study can make deliberately: sensitive mode raises the empirical target to 0.20 and recovers 72% of single-read mutations on J_{H}4 and 87% on N114 V_{H} at observed FDR 0.18 and 0.16, while events unresolved individually can be estimated in aggregate. Together, these outputs make the sensitivity–background tradeoff explicit and help investigators choose a calling regime appropriate to their experiment.

A shared framework accommodates four platforms through distinct analyses: control-calibrated read-library substitutions, direct Sanger counting and PacBio junction characterization. A Sanger clone is one molecule read once, so its mutations are read directly; the workflow therefore counts quality-filtered substitutions directly and reports the control-derived background separately. Nanopore and Illumina libraries carry matched AID-null arms, so each candidate is tested against the errors those controls make. Nanopore spans the whole amplicon, which made the 3′ decline visible without tiling, identified clones from the V(D)J junction and ordered their mutations into trees; Illumina gives depth without that linkage. PacBio combines length with per-base accuracy, and reading the donor, the join and the acceptor on one molecule recovered a known end-joining phenotype from a deposit with no AID-null arm, using junction shape in place of calibrated calls. Figure S21 sets out what each measured. Agreement between platforms is itself evidence: nanopore and Sanger reads from the same six mice gave substitution spectra correlated at r = 0.92 and the same AID-null contrast, supporting the use of cloning-free long reads to recover established SHM patterns while adding whole-amplicon linkage.

Reanalysis of public data tested SHMtrace against independent biological and counting expectations. Published sequence collections support reproducible reanalysis when clones are linked to animals and genotypes: SHMtrace recovered the principal J_{H}4 frequency and G:C comparisons, matched SW_{HEL} counts across five arms and twelve substitution types (2,804 of 2,809 tabulated mutations; r = 1.0000), and recovered 150 of 150 mutations an antibody-evolution study curated by hand, calling none its authors did not. Agreement was not complete, and the exceptions are as informative: Wt frequencies differed after denominator harmonisation, the published *Mkln1* A:T decrease was not significant in the reanalysis, and the SW_{HEL} total-mutation inference differed under a different test (Tables S4 and S5; Figures S14–S16). The Sanger re-analysis also surfaced bookkeeping problems, which SHMtrace now checks and reports rather than silently resolving. By retaining assignments, counting conventions and quality flags, SHMtrace makes legacy sequence collections easier to revisit, compare and reuse.

What this enables in practice is a short experiment-to-answer path that keeps its own evidence: read accounting, fixed-seed sampling and one engine behind the desktop and command-line interfaces, with every exclusion, threshold and flagged site written out for inspection. The power analysis sets the honest expectation. How many animals a study needs depends on the difference it seeks: under variation estimated from three Wt J_{H}4 mice, the AID-competent against AID-null contrasts made here separate at three animals per group, whereas a two-fold difference between two AID-proficient groups would require approximately twenty, with considerable uncertainty in that estimate (Figure S8B). Sequencing depth cannot substitute for independent biological samples, and no analysis can recover a comparison the design did not support. Within that boundary the framework extends readily. It needs only a target, matched AID-null libraries and a sample sheet, supporting its application to off-target loci, switch regions, engineered diversification and base-editor substrates across twelve loci from eleven laboratories. Further validation on independently prepared runs and UMI-tagged SHM libraries can establish how far this framework extends. SHMtrace brings control-calibrated mutation detection, whole-amplicon profiles and comparisons across biological replicates into one reproducible workflow. By preserving mutation linkage and documenting analytical choices, it makes long-read SHM data more informative and supports transparent reanalysis of existing sequence collections. This provides a practical foundation for studying how AID targeting and DNA repair shape antibody diversification.

## Proposed STAR Methods additions

Integrate these into their existing Methods subsections rather than adding a separate prose supplement. Additional supporting details to retain are listed in DETAIL_RELOCATION_LEDGER.md.

### Sanger counting and provenance

For the matched-platform comparison, 184 traces were assigned to the six mice, 2 excluded in the sample sheet and 182 processed; 48 failed analysis and 134 were retained (Figure S13). This distinguishes the complete 447-trace archive from the matched comparison.

### Caller operating ranges

On real J_{H}4 libraries, ClairS reported one mutation and DeepSomatic at most 13, compared with 221, 344 and 73 SHMtrace calls; N114 totals were one, at most 14, and 70, 49 and 173, respectively. ClairS rejected both Wt3 candidates supported by ≥20% of reads. Among 1,197 retained calls, 53% were in <1%, 93% in <5% and 99.5% in <20% of reads. These results describe the tested settings and datasets rather than universal caller limits.

### Sanger comparison details

In matched mice, G:C hotspot shares were 58% for Sanger and 54% for nanopore versus 20% of eligible positions; A:T shares were 70% and 69% versus 50%. No equivalence conclusion follows from the non-significant interaction at n=3. In the independent SW_{HEL} comparison, the analysis window was 558 bp; per-class p-values were 9.7 × 10^{−11}, 7.7 × 10^{−6}, 1.2 × 10^{−10} and 0.14. The published total-mutation p=0.07 used one-way ANOVA; the reanalysis p=3.8 × 10^{−4} used per-animal log-rate Welch. The deposit's 12 hosts differ from the reported 13; the potentially pooled host was retained and excluded in sensitivity analysis. A:T mutation shares fell from 56% in Wt to 5% in *Msh2* mutants.

### PacBio junction comparison details

Mean-microhomology comparisons against Wt gave p=0.92 for *Shld1*, 0.0092 for *Xlf* and 9.3 × 10^{−5} for the double mutant. The ≥4-nt share rose from 7% to 18% (p=0.0053), and blunt junctions fell from 21% to 7% (p=0.0028). *Shld1* insertion-bearing junctions were 48% versus 31% in Wt (p=0.0074). Reads per unique junction ranged 1.1–23.3; inferred structures do not measure switching efficiency.

### Two-candidate example

At Sμ WT3, position 165 (TACCT) had 20 of 2,000 supporting reads, median Q34; position 97 (AGCTT) had 15 of 1,941, median Q33. Both had support on both strands. KO1 carried the latter in 14 of 1,879 reads at median Q33. Geneious called it in two controls and all three cases. These examples distinguish control-supported calling decisions, without independent truth validation of each native candidate.

### Real-library caller details

Figure 4's unique-density separation ranged 16–62-fold for Geneious and 2–40-fold across LoFreq settings. J_{H}4 Wt calls were 221, 344 and 73 versus 0, 2 and 0 in nulls; Geneious gave 203, 337 and 73 versus 2, 6 and 5. Default LoFreq gave 0, 11 and 0; BAQ-off gave 22 in Wt2. External Sμ Wt counts were 65–157 for SHMtrace and 52–131 for Geneious, with 0–2 versus 2–10 per null. The displayed Wt/null raster libraries had 34/35 other Q≥20 mismatches after germline exclusion; Wt added 5,643 mismatches at called sites. These are individual mismatch observations, distinct from unique variant counts.

### Maea comparison details

SHMtrace processed 293 chromatograms in 16 s after manual assignment curation. G:C transitions fell from 19% to 11% (same published shares; p=2.9 × 10^{−4} versus 0.0028 published), while transversions rose from 15% to 21% versus 14% to 20% published (p=0.025 versus 0.0285). Mouse rankings correlated at ρ=0.95. Controls had two mutations in 19 clones (p=3.0 × 10^{−4} versus Wt and 0.0039 versus knockout). Shared trace assignments and 134 identical mutated-clone pairs remained as filed and were flagged.

### Ramos control sensitivity

The noisy null library carried 36 substitutions, 61% at G:C, while the other two had none. Retaining it as a decoy suppressed all sample substitution calls. Excluding it from decoys, while retaining it in model fitting, yielded 86–97% G:C calls; treating it as a sample instead yielded 71–84%. The rule was developed from this dataset, so this is a sensitivity analysis rather than independent validation of that rule.

### mOrange raw-background comparison

The source study's Q>20 every-mismatch counts gave inactive-AID background of 0.31–0.72 per 10^{3} bases, approximately two-fold active/inactive separation in the empty arm, and 43–66% G:C transitions in inactive backgrounds. SHMtrace's inactive Ugi and UNG arms retained 0 and 2 calls, respectively. Calling across contexts used inactive-AID empty-arm controls. The paired effect and interval use one transition-share value per experiment; the pooled 95%/4% shares have a different weighting and need not subtract to the paired mean difference.

### Somatic caller spike-in false calls

Across the four planted datasets, permissive DeepSomatic produced at most 0.4 false calls per library under the evaluated settings.
