SHMtrace calls somatic hypermutation and other AID-driven mutation with a measured false-discovery rate

Summary

Cloned-amplicon Sanger sequencing provides accurate but low-throughput measurements of somatic hypermutation (SHM). We developed SHMtrace to quantify substitutions and indels against background measured in matched activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID)-deficient controls and compare mutation profiles across biological replicates. A Sanger workflow quantifies substitutions and reports control-derived background separately. Evaluation included planted mutations, recalled at measured false-discovery rates of 0.050 and 0.045, simulated lineages, and twelve loci from eleven laboratories across four sequencing platforms. Across five nanopore assays, SHMtrace reduced substitution calls in AID-null controls by 64–90% relative to the uncorrected Geneious rule. In four assays, AID-active and AID-null libraries showed 66–141-fold separation in unique-variant density under the stated zero-call convention. Nanopore reads preserved mutation linkage across the amplicon and yielded substitution spectra similar to Sanger sequencing of the same mice. Further datasets extended analysis to engineered diversification and class-switch junctions. SHMtrace provides a reproducible framework for control-informed analysis of AID-associated diversification.

Keywords

somatic hypermutation, activation-induced cytidine deaminase, nanopore sequencing, variant calling, negative controls, false-discovery rate, pseudoreplication, immunoglobulin, mutation trees, Sanger sequencing

Introduction

Somatic hypermutation (SHM) diversifies immunoglobulin (Ig) genes in germinal-center B cells. It is initiated by Activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) which deaminates cytosines in Ig DNA. Replication over the resulting uracils, together with uracil excision and mismatch repair, generates substitutions at G:C and A:T base pairs1–3. Because each repair pathway leaves its own footprint, the frequency, position and spectrum of mutations are therefore key readouts of AID activity and broader lesion processing4–7. Relatedly, the mouse JH4 downstream intron is widely used as a genomic reporter because it undergoes SHM without directly contributing to antigen binding8,9.

AID activity read out from JH4 amplicons can be measured on various sequencing platforms, each reporting a different kind of evidence. Cloned-amplicon Sanger sequencing reads one molecule at a time at high per-base confidence, though it yields only tens of sequences per animal10. Short reads typical of Illumina sequencers give depth but may not span the complete region11. Long reads from ONT and PacBio span the JH4, so mutations stay linked across the amplicon, and nanopore accuracy now reaches modal values above 99% with R10.4.1 flow cells12–14; nanopore sequencing has also been used to sequence Ig genes, where consensus sequencing can absorb errors15,16. What all platforms, however, share is the need to separate AID-driven mutation from their own error. A wild-type JH4 intron carries roughly one mutation per 100 bases, whereas the residual error of a single filtered read, especially with nanopore, is within one or two orders of magnitude of that rate and is concentrated at particular positions and sequence contexts17.

Two common analysis practices make this problem worse. First, general-purpose low-frequency variant callers judge each base by its own quality score18, and the simple rules used in commercial software test candidates one at a time without correcting for the number of tests19. In the configurations evaluated here, neither measures the errors a particular library makes, so mismatches that recur at one position, from PCR, sequence context or a co-amplified paralog, can be counted as SHM mutations. Second, treating reads from the same animal as independent replicates overstates statistical certainty because multiple reads can derive from the same B-cell clone or PCR-amplified molecule20. Matched AID-null libraries address technical background, whereas group comparisons must account for biological replication by using one summary value per animal. Existing SHM software summarises previously identified mutations21–23 but does not use matched AID-null controls to distinguish candidate variants from technical background.

Here we describe SHMtrace, which integrates variant calling calibrated against matched AID-null controls from the same sequencing run with mutation profiling and group comparisons across independent biological replicates, starting from delivered reads or Sanger chromatograms. We validated it with planted mutations, simulated lineages, twelve loci from eleven laboratories across four platforms, Sanger sequencing of the same mice, and deposited clones from three published Sanger studies, and compared it against LoFreq18, the long-read somatic callers ClairS24 and DeepSomatic25, the germline callers Clair326 and DeepVariant27, and the uncorrected Geneious test19.

Results

SHMtrace calibrates substitutions against AID-null controls

SHMtrace starts from the files that a sequencing provider or core facility returns (Figures 1 and S1). Nanopore and Illumina reads are first filtered by length and expected error28 and aligned with minimap229 against the target together with competing references. Reads that align better to a paralog, are chimeric, or carry ten or more paralog-specific bases are removed (Figure 1B), and the same number of usable reads is then drawn at random from every library. Sanger chromatograms are assigned to animals through a sample sheet and trimmed to their high-quality core, and reads tagged with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) are collapsed to one consensus per molecule.

The central step is the variant test (Figures 1A and 1C). For each substitution type, strand, and base quality, SHMtrace measures how often the AID-null libraries of the same run show that change, and asks whether a candidate is supported by more reads, at the qualities observed, than this background explains. Because quality scores overstate certainty at the highest qualities (Figure S2), the control rates are multiplied by a margin chosen by leaving one control out at a time (STAR Methods). The held-out controls then serve as decoys, in an approach inspired by target–decoy analysis29: SHMtrace keeps the most permissive cutoff at which estimated false calls are at most 5% of calls. This is an empirical estimate targeting 5%, not a guarantee: it relies on a few controls representing the sample's background, and the same controls also choose the margin. Candidates arise only where a read already differs, so the set tested is data-dependent, and its null p-values need not be superuniform, as Benjamini–Hochberg30 requires; SHMtrace counts false calls in controls selected the same way. Alternative alleles carried by at least half the reads at a position are set aside as likely germline or parental differences. This is a heuristic, not a test: a mutation shared by a lineage that dominates a library crosses the same threshold, so every site it removes is reported with its read count and frequency.

The read-library test can retain a candidate supported by a single read; repeated sequencing of the same original molecule is not required. SHMtrace uses a separate Sanger counting route (Figure 1C): every substitution at base quality ≥ 20 is counted, and the AID-null clones' mismatch rate gives the expected background count, reported separately without subtraction. The germline filter, window, automatic checks and animal-level comparisons are shared. The Sanger results below therefore test trace reading, counting, bookkeeping and group comparisons, not the control-calibrated test.

Two C-to-T candidates in an external wild-type Sμ library illustrate how matched controls distinguish candidate mutations from recurrent background (WT3; Figure 2). The substitution at position 165, within a WRC hotspot (TACCT), was supported by 20 of 2,000 reads, compared with 15 of 1,941 reads at position 97 (AGCTT). Both had support on both strands and similar median base qualities (Q34 and Q33, respectively). However, no control supported the position-165 substitution at Q≥20, whereas KO1 carried the position-97 substitution in 14 of 1,879 reads at a median quality of Q33. SHMtrace retained the position-165 candidate (p = 5 × 10−19) and rejected the recurrent position-97 candidate (p = 1), which the Geneious rule called in two controls and all three wild-type libraries. Across WT3, 743 distinct substitutions were evaluated as potential mutations, of which 88 met the control-calibrated calling threshold (p ≤ 0.009; Figure 2D). The remaining candidates could not be distinguished confidently from background.

SHMtrace compares groups using one summary value per biological replicate, with Welch tests on log-transformed rates, negative-binomial tests with parametric bootstrapping, and permutation tests (Figure 1D; Figure S3 shows each output with an example). It characterizes mutation patterns through substitution spectra, positional clustering, hotspot use and an S5F-based targeting index23. Additional analyses estimate single-read mutations that could not be called individually, group reads into clones by their junction sequences, reconstruct read-level mutation trees and estimate statistical power. The command-line and desktop interfaces run the same analysis engine.

Planted mutations and simulated lineages quantify recall and false calls

We planted 60 AID-weighted substitutions into each held-out control library, preserving base qualities and assigning 1–20 supporting reads per site, with three repetitions per control (Figure 3). On JH4, SHMtrace recalled 81% overall, and 95% of mutations were supported by at least three reads, with 2.6 false calls per library (observed FDR 0.050). In contrast, Geneious recalled more single-read mutations (60% versus 31%) but produced 4.9 false calls per library (FDR 0.083). Default LoFreq recalled none; disabling its short-read-oriented base-alignment quality correction yielded 34% recall, restricted to mutations with at least five supporting reads. Permissive settings increased recall to 56% with 4.4 false calls per library (Figures 3A and 3D).

ClairS24 and DeepSomatic25 were supplied with the other AID-null libraries as matched normals. Neither recovered planted mutations at default settings. Across 2,160 substitutions on JH4, N114 VH, Sμ and Pim1, permissive ClairS recovered none; permissive DeepSomatic recalled 19%, 18%, 5% and 3%, respectively, with at most 0.4 false calls per library. Recall increased at higher allele fractions, approaching complete recovery at 20% on JH4 (Figure 3C). On real JH4 libraries, ClairS reported one mutation and DeepSomatic at most 13 across three mice, compared with 221, 344 and 73 SHMtrace calls. Corresponding N114 VH totals were one and at most 14, compared with 70, 49 and 173. ClairS rejected both Wt3 candidates supported by at least 20% of reads. Most SHMtrace-retained calls had much lower fractions: among 1,197 calls across four loci, 53% occurred in <1% of reads, 93% in <5% and 99.5% in <20% (Figure 2E). Germline callers Clair3 and DeepVariant recovered none of the 2,160 planted substitutions, consistent with their intended operating range (Table S1).

On N114 VH, SHMtrace recalled 90% of planted substitutions, including 58% supported by one read, with 2.6 false calls per library (FDR 0.045; Figure 3B). Geneious recalled 88% with 2.8 false calls (FDR 0.050); LoFreq without base-alignment quality recalled 34%. On external Sμ and Pim1 controls at 2,000 reads, SHMtrace recalled 63% and 72% at FDR 0.055 and 0.035. Geneious recalled 59% and 64% at FDR 0.114 and 0.050; LoFreq recalled 0–26%, with permissive FDR 0.27–0.52 (Figure 3D).

Calling the same libraries at targets from 1% to 30% tested whether the threshold delivers the rate it targets. At the default 5%, the pooled false-discovery proportion was 3.5–5.5% across the four loci, and looser targets stayed at or below their stated rate except JH4 at 10% (13.8%) (Figure S4A). Targets below 5% did not deliver their stated rate on JH4, Sμ or Pim1 (2.0–6.1% at a 1% target; N114 VH, 0.5%): false calls per library levelled off while recall fell, from 81% to 17% on JH4 (Figures S4B and S4C).

Varying the controls through the whole procedure on JH4 showed how many are needed (Figure S5). One control leaves no decoys, so the threshold falls back to Benjamini–Hochberg: it recalled 68% of planted lineage mutations, against 93% with two controls and 95% with three or four, at false-discovery proportions of 2.8–3.1%. All-null libraries yielded no calls with any number of controls (40 runs). One control carrying recurrent artifacts changed false calls little, with or without the noisy-control rule. On Sμ, AID-null controls from a second experiment performed like the matched controls (66–68% versus 63% recall), and pooling all five lowered false calls from 2.2 to 0.8 per library.

Diluting real hypermutated reads into AID-null reads tested the estimates on mutations AID made, with their clonality and clustering (Figure S6). On N114 VH, mutation frequency was 0.87–0.97 of the expected value at 25–50% donor reads, and calls were made from 1–5% donor reads. On JH4, detection was all-or-nothing and depended on the donor: calls began at 2% donor reads for Wt3 and at 50% for Wt2, and no call was made below that.

Indel validation used ten planted insertions or deletions of 1–40 bp per library, supported by 1–20 reads, with eight draws per control and error models fitted to the other controls. Among events supported by at least three reads, recall was 72% on JH4 and 91% on N114 VH, with 0.33 and zero false calls per library. Events supported by one or two reads were excluded by the three-read requirement. JH4 single-base homopolymer deletions were least reliably recovered (19%); recall was 100% for 2-bp insertions and 89% for deletions ≥10 bp (Figure S7).

We next simulated approximately 40 B-cell clones per JH4 library, with clone sizes following a power-law distribution. Mutations were introduced at positions sampled according to S5F mutability, including mutations shared across an entire clone, restricted to individual branches, or unique to individual sequences (Figure 3E). SHMtrace recalled 83% of single-read mutations versus 57% for Geneious, with FDR 0.037 versus 0.012 and 13.7 versus 4.0 false calls per library. In null libraries, false calls averaged 0.7 versus 4.3. Junction clustering recovered clone assignments with adjusted Rand indices31 of 0.94–0.98. When an unmutated clone contributed 60% of reads, read-weighted frequency was 6.2 versus a true 6.0 per 103 bp, while the majority-rule clone-weighted measure recovered 94% of its corresponding true burden (14.6 versus 15.5; Figure 3F).

The dominant unmutated clone could not trigger the ≥50% allele filter. Repeating the simulation with a mutated dominant clone showed no loss at 40% abundance, but all mutations crossing the threshold at 60% or 80% were removed. Read-weighted and clone-weighted estimates fell by as much as 62% and 34%, respectively (STAR Methods). Excluded sites are reported for inspection.

At 500 reads per library, read-weighted mutation frequencies were within 2% of the simulated values for both JH4 and N114 VH (Figure 3G). Unique-variant density was less consistently recovered: JH4 estimates were 97% of the true density at 500 reads and 94% at 1,000 reads, whereas N114 VH reached 77% at 1,000 reads. This endpoint counts each distinct substitution once and divides by the number of analyzed bases. As sequencing depth increases, additional reads can carry mutations already counted, increasing the denominator without adding new variants; depth also affects detection of rare mutations. Including the aggregate estimate of single-read mutations that were not called individually improved recovery to 96–100% of the true density across the simulated scenarios. In a separate test, planting 25, 50 or 100 single-read mutations increased this estimate by an average of 95% of the number introduced (Figure S8A). Comparisons of unique-variant density therefore require matched sequencing depth.

Group-test calibration was assessed in 300 simulations per condition, with between-animal coefficient of variation 1 (Figure 3H). Under no true difference, pooled-read tests returned p < 0.05 in 91% of 3-versus-3 and 94% of 5-versus-5 experiments. Negative-binomial rejection rates were 5.0% and 5.7%. With three animals per group, that test detected two-fold and four-fold changes in 12% and 30% of simulations, illustrating the limited power at this sample size.

Matched controls set the background in real libraries

We next compared callers on real libraries with matched AID-null controls (Table S2; Figure 4). These included our JH4 intron libraries from germinal-center B cells of three wild-type (Wt) and three Aicda−/− mice, N114 hybridoma subclones transduced with AID or empty vector, and three loci from an independent laboratory's public nanopore dataset: the Sμ switch region in LPS-activated B cells and in CH12 cells, and the AID off-target gene Pim1. Every caller analysed exactly the same reads (Table S3).

SHMtrace retained 1–4 calls across the three controls per assay, versus 5–20 for Geneious (Figures 4A–4E and 4G). Geometric-mean unique-density separation, with zero-call controls assigned one-event values, was 141-fold for JH4, 85-fold for N114 VH (Figure S9), 66-fold for primary-cell Sμ and 94-fold for CH12 Sμ, compared with 16–62-fold for Geneious and 2–40-fold for LoFreq configurations (Figure 4F). JH4 Wt counts were 221, 344 and 73 versus 0, 2 and 0 in controls; Geneious yielded 203, 337 and 73 versus 2, 6 and 5. Default LoFreq called 0, 11 and 0 Wt variants. Disabling base-alignment quality yielded 22 in Wt2 versus 221 for SHMtrace. At 500 reads, JH4 and N114 VH had reached 90% and 59% of calls observed at the largest tested depth (Figure S10), without establishing saturation. External Sμ Wt counts were 65–157 for SHMtrace and 52–131 for Geneious, with 0–2 versus 2–10 per control. Control mismatches were position-dependent (Figures S11A and S11B). After germline exclusion, representative Wt and null JH4 libraries contained 34 and 35 other high-quality mismatches, while the Wt library added 5,643 at called sites, often shared within clones (Figures S11C and S11D).

The same N114 VH reads were analyzed by IgBLAST32 and SHazaM22, which count every mismatch to the germline (Figure S12). With the IMGT germline the AID libraries were 2-fold above the empty-vector libraries, because the parental line differs from it (99.3% identity) and sequencing errors count as mutations. Using the parental V as germline raised this to 6-fold, and masking bases below Q20 to 32-fold, compared with 188-fold for SHMtrace.

At Pim1, mutation calls in AID-competent libraries remained close to the AID-null background. At 2,000 reads per library, SHMtrace yielded 1.8-fold separation between groups, with four calls across the controls, compared with 1.6-fold separation and eleven control calls for Geneious. Increasing depth to all available reads (3,238–3,447 per library) did not clearly resolve the signal: SHMtrace detected 3–10 variants per AID-competent library and 0–3 per AID-null library, while Geneious detected 5–7 and 0–4, respectively (Table S1).

The two CH12 AID off-target loci, off-target 1 and off-target 2, tested a genetic perturbation expected to reduce AID targeting33. Off-target 1 Wt libraries carried 69, 50, and 55 calls versus 0, 2, and 0 in Aicda−/− controls, with densities of 3.07 versus 0.04 per 105 bases (Welch p = 9.7 × 10−4 on log density; 0.024 on frequency). Off-target 2 counts were 42, 28, and 23 versus 2, 0, and 0, with densities of 1.68 versus 0.04 (p = 5.5 × 10−4 and 0.030). G:C substitutions accounted for 97% of 174 off-target 1 calls and 97% of 93 off-target 2 calls. MLLT1/MLLT3 double knockout reduced counts to 2, 0, and 2 at off-target 1 (0.07 per 105 bases) and 1, 0, and 0 at off-target 2 (0.02), statistically indistinguishable from Aicda−/− controls (p = 0.51 and 0.43). No fold is reported because a zero-count convention would determine its magnitude.

SHMtrace multiplies the control-derived error rates by an empirically selected calibration factor to account for background that the fitted model underestimates. This adjustment increases the expected error probability used in the test, so a candidate requires stronger evidence to be called; it does not change the observed mutation count. The factor is selected by testing each control against the remaining controls and choosing the smallest tested multiplier for which no more than 1% of held-out candidate tests yield p < 0.01. Without adjustment, 5.4% of JH4 and 9.6% of N114 VH held-out tests fell below this threshold. Factors of five for the shared JH4 window and ten for full-length JH4 and N114 VH reduced these proportions to 1.0%, 0.7% and 0.5%, respectively (Figure 4H). These factors are empirical calibration adjustments: they do not imply that sequencing errors are universally five or ten times higher than the fitted model, and they do not by themselves deliver a 5% false-discovery rate, which the decoy threshold below addresses separately.

Read preparation excluded 44–79% of delivered JH4 reads per library, or 67% overall. Expected-error filtering and competitive alignment accounted for 29% and 25% of delivered reads, respectively (Figure 4I). Competitive alignment alone did not remove all paralog-derived reads: full-length paralog reads could align preferentially to the longer target reference when the competing paralog reference was shorter. Without an additional filter based on paralog-specific bases, these reads generated 35–54 false variant calls per simulated library.

SHMtrace-generated nanopore and Sanger profiles agree in matched mice

SHMtrace processed 182 Sanger chromatograms from the six nanopore-sequenced mice, retaining 134 clones after alignment and sequence-quality filtering for comparison across the shared 529-bp window (Figures 5 and S13). SHMtrace-derived mutation profiles distinguished Wt from Aicda−/− mice on both platforms: Sanger showed 38-fold separation (p = 8.8 × 10−4), and nanopore showed 141-fold separation when zero-call controls were assigned one event (p = 0.0027; Welch tests on log densities, n = 3 mice per group). Pooled substitution spectra were similar across the twelve substitution types (descriptive Pearson r = 0.92; slope = 0.97), as were positional profiles in 25-bp bins (r = 0.94). Among G:C mutations, 58% of Sanger and 54% of nanopore calls occurred at WRC/GYW hotspots, which accounted for 20% of eligible sites in the window. Among A:T mutations, 70% and 69%, respectively, occurred at WA/TW motifs, compared with 50% of eligible sites. No platform-by-substitution-type interaction was detected (p = 0.62), although this does not establish equivalence with three mice per group. Absolute unique-variant densities are not directly comparable between workflows because the Sanger analysis counts mutations in individual cloned products, whereas the nanopore analysis counts distinct variants among 500 reads.

Sanger reanalysis recovers published comparisons and identifies discrepancies

A published JH4 study compared four Wt and four Maeafl/flMb1cre mice with Aicda−/− controls using SHMTool21,34. After reconstruction of clone assignments, SHMtrace processed 293 chromatograms in 16 s; while curation took substantially longer (Figure S14; STAR Methods). Mutation frequency was 4.6-fold lower in Maea-deficient mice versus 4.4-fold in the publication (one-tailed t tests, p = 2.3 × 10−4 and 0.0143). G:C transitions fell from 19% to 11% (published, 19% to 11%; p = 2.9 × 10−4 and 0.0028), and transversions rose from 15% to 21% (14% to 20%; p = 0.025 and 0.0285). Spectra correlated at r = 0.99; mouse rankings at ρ = 0.95. Under the published clone-count × 577-bp convention, frequencies averaged 1.0-fold the published values. Controls carried two mutations in 19 clones (p = 3.0 × 10−4 and 0.0039 versus Wt and knockout). Four traces assigned to two mice and 134 identical mutated-clone pairs were retained as filed and flagged.

Reanalysis of 402 Mkln1-study chromatograms34 found an 8.0-fold frequency reduction versus the published 7.8-fold (p = 0.0038 and 0.014). G:C transversions increased from 15% to 29% versus 16% to 27% in the published tables (p = 0.0078 and 0.0040). Spectrum correlations were r = 0.97 for Wt and 0.62 for knockout (Figure S15; Table S4).

Both of those studies come from the laboratory that produced the libraries analysed here, so we also re-analysed a Sanger study from an unrelated laboratory, at a different locus: Thientosapol et al. hypermutated a SWHEL VH10 knock-in V region in germinal-centre B cells and reported that inactivating Samhd1 raises transitions at C:G base pairs and lowers transversions at A:T and at C:G in both strands35. Its 1,795 deposited clones were analysed from their own records alone, with the germline taken as their majority consensus (STAR Methods); the 558-bp window this yields matches the 560 bp the study analyzed. Across the five arms and the twelve substitution types, SHMtrace counted 2,804 of the 2,809 mutations the study tabulates (Pearson r = 1.0000 over the 60 cells; Figure S16), and mutations per sequence agreed to two decimal places in every arm. The four substitution classes the study tested moved as reported: transversions fell at A:T (p = 9.7 × 10−11) and at C:G (p = 7.7 × 10−6), transitions at C:G rose (p = 1.2 × 10−10), and transitions at A:T did not move (p = 0.14). For total mutation the study reported a decrease that did not reach significance (p = 0.07, one-way ANOVA); a Welch test on per-animal log frequency, a different test, found it lower (p = 3.8 × 10−4), so that comparison agrees in direction but not in inference. The deposit names 12 wild-type hosts where the study reports 13, one of them carrying about twice the clones of any other. That host is retained, because nothing in the records says which clone came from which animal; every animal-level test was repeated without it and none changed (STAR Methods). The Msh2−/− arm, which SHMtrace was not told was a repair mutant, showed the expected collapse of A:T mutations, from 56% to 5% of all mutations. Because this study deposits sequences rather than chromatograms, it tests the calling, the spectrum and the group statistics but not the trace reading.

Long reads retain whole-amplicon profiles and within-lineage linkage

Nanopore sequencing extended SHMtrace analysis of the JH4 intron from the shared 529-bp Sanger window to a 1,141-bp interval spanning positions 90–1,230 (Figure 6A). Across this longer interval, Wt unique-variant density was 229-fold above controls. Approximately 69% of mutations occurred within the first 450 bp, with a seven-fold decline between the first and last 250 bp, consistent with the reported decline downstream of V(D)J9. Substitution spectra across the full interval closely matched those within the Sanger window (Pearson r = 0.989; Figure S17).

Wt mutations preferentially occurred at positions predicted to undergo SHM by the S5F model, which assigns mutability according to the surrounding five-base sequence. The targeting index, defined as mean predicted mutability at mutated positions divided by the mean across the analysis window, ranged from 1.22 to 1.36, indicating 22–36% enrichment over the window average (permutation p ≤ 0.015). Calls per position were also 4.1-fold higher in the highest-mutability quintile than in the lowest (Figure 6B). In contrast, Aicda−/− mismatch positions showed no enrichment (index = 1.00; p = 0.50). Because S5F mutability is not used in the variant-calling test, this analysis provides a separate check that the detected mutations follow an SHM-associated targeting pattern.

Junction sequences upstream of JH4 group reads into B-cell lineages (Figure 6C). In Wt2, one largely unmutated lineage accounted for 81% of junction-assigned reads. Read-weighted frequency was 2.8 per 103 bp, compared with 16.3 when each lineage's majority-rule mutation profile was counted once. Wt3 and Wt4 changed less (22.5 to 24.9 and 4.9 to 5.3). The largest Wt3 lineage comprised 69 reads and four founder mutations, with five inferred sublineages extending to 17 mutations (Figure 6D). 70% of mutated reads fitted one tree; 7/1,128 mutation pairs failed the four-gamete test. Without UMIs, PCR recombination and residual errors remain possible, so these are read-level reconstructions rather than proven cellular lineages.

Public datasets test transfer across platforms and experimental designs

Three public Illumina studies tested different biological contexts (Figure 7). At JH4 positions 53–122, Wt mice showed 56-fold greater mutation frequency than AID-null mice (log-rate Welch p = 0.005; n = 7 and 4). Ldha deletion showed no detectable difference from Wt (Mann–Whitney p = 0.75), consistent with the original study36 (Figure 7A).

In Aicda−/−Ung−/−Msh2−/− CH12F3 cells, ectopic AID increased frequency 40-fold over empty vector (p = 0.00781; n = 4 each), with one control call in 20,000 reads37. All AID calls were C→T/G→A, consistent with loss of uracil excision and mismatch repair; R50E yielded none (Figure 7B). R174E/R178E appeared more active but gave only 17% transitions, a 56% cluster within positions 164–172 and targeting index 0.73 versus 2.46 for AID. These features suggest a library-specific artifact absent from controls (Figure 7C). Establishing its source would require additional sequencing provenance or independent material.

HMCES-deficient Ramos clones had 3.8-fold more deletions and 2.5-fold more insertions than Wt clones (deletions: p = 1.0 × 10−4; n = 6 per group)38 (Figure 7D). Re-analysis of the deposit, which the original study does not report, found substitution calling sensitive to one of its three AID-knockout controls, which carried 36 substitutions, 61% at G:C, while the other two had none. Including this library among the decoys used to select the calling threshold prevented any substitutions from being called in the Wt or HMCES-deficient clones. SHMtrace’s noisy-control rule therefore excluded it from threshold selection, while retaining it in background-model fitting and documenting the exclusion. With this adjustment, Wt and HMCES-deficient clones yielded 52–99 substitution calls each, with 86–97% at G:C. In a sensitivity analysis, treating the noisy library as a sample rather than a control yielded 277–413 calls per Wt or HMCES-deficient clone, with 71–84% at G:C. The difference shows that control assignment materially affects the inferred substitution burden. The same exclusion rule was triggered in the mOrange analysis.

On dual-UMI R10 rRNA-operon reads39, consensus reduced error 57-fold (95% CI 35–99) to 0.17%, with residual errors concentrated in homopolymer indels. At ≥10 reads per family, substitution error approached one per 10,000 bases (Figure S8D). Critically, this is a technical consensus benchmark rather than an SHM experiment.

A published mOrange reporter study compared AID-ER with catalytically inactive AID-E58Q-ER in NIH-3T3 cells carrying an empty vector, Ugi (Uracil-DNA glycosylase inhibitor) or UNG (Uracil DNA glycosylase), using 36-bp sequencing reads40. Counting all mismatches at Q>20 yielded background frequencies of 0.31–0.72 per 103 bases in inactive-AID libraries, with only two-fold separation between active and inactive AID in the empty-vector condition. SHMtrace used the inactive-AID libraries from this condition to calibrate substitution calling across all three repair conditions. It retained 115 substitutions across nine active-AID libraries, all at G:C bases, with 75–100% at WRC/GYW hotspots. Inactive-AID libraries carrying Ugi or UNG retained zero and two calls, respectively. Separation between active and inactive AID increased to 20-fold with empty vector, 1,002-fold with Ugi and 103-fold with UNG; the Ugi comparison assigned the zero-call controls one event. Among retained substitutions, the transition share decreased from 95% with Ugi to 4% with UNG, whereas uncorrected inactive-AID backgrounds contained 43–66% transitions (Figure 7E). The mean paired difference between Ugi and UNG was 92 percentage points (95% CI 74–110; p = 0.0021), although the small number of calls per library limits precision.

Two engineered-diversification datasets extended the evaluation to mutations measured relative to experimental starting constructs (Figure S18). In a study using a common GFP substrate41, no-editor libraries yielded at most one call in 20,000 reads. AID substitutions were more frequently located at WRC/GYW hotspots than APOBEC3A substitutions, both in Wt cells (56% versus 14%; p = 0.0040) and in UNG/MSH2-deficient cells (37% versus 20%; p = 9.0 × 10−4). In the repair-deficient background, however, their transition shares were similar (78% versus 76%; p = 0.74), showing that transition enrichment alone could not distinguish the two enzymes.

The second engineered dataset, a CRISPR-X antibody-evolution study, diversified an antibody VH and published the mutations it found in each selected cluster42. Comparing the two lists position by position, SHMtrace recovered 150 of the 150 published mutations and called none the authors did not, across all 42 cluster entries (Figure S18B). Because the comparator is an independently curated list rather than another run of the same software, this is the sharpest available test of variant identity on this route.

Two further datasets tested transfer to another laboratory's switch-region libraries and to a fourth platform, and showed where a deposit limits what can be asked. Sμ libraries under PI3Kδ inhibition43 confound genotype with library layout: six of nine wild-type libraries are single-end whereas every Aicda−/− library is paired, and the mates do not overlap, so only the twelve paired libraries reach the switch repeats where AID acts (Figure S19). Analysed over those repeats, the nine Aicda−/− libraries returned one call between them against 81–172 per wild-type library (p = 0.0021), and the wild-type calls carried the AID signature: targeting index 1.52–1.67 and 31–35% C→T or G→A. The calibration therefore transfers to an independent laboratory, platform and perturbation. The drug comparison does not follow from this deposit, because only one wild-type replicate per treatment reaches the repeats.

PacBio Sμ–Sγ1 junctions44 read the donor, the join and the acceptor on one 6,126-bp molecule, so the junction module was applied to a class-switch phenotype it was not tuned for (Figure S20). Mean microhomology was 1.01 nt in wild type and unchanged in Shld1−/− (1.02 nt; p = 0.92), but rose without XLF (1.69 nt; p = 0.0092) and in the double mutant (1.59 nt; p = 9.3 × 10−5), where junctions with ≥4 nt of microhomology rose from 7% to 18% (p = 0.0053) and blunt junctions fell from 21% to 7% (p = 0.0028); Shld1−/− junctions instead carried more inserted bases (48% versus 31%; p = 0.0074). This is the shift toward microhomology-mediated end joining expected when classical non-homologous end joining is impaired, with no parameter fitted to the dataset. Reads per unique junction vary several-fold between samples (1.1–23.3), so junction counts measure long-range PCR recovery rather than switching efficiency; junction shape, not calibrated calls, is compared, and re-aligning each acceptor segment on its own places 19–47% of unique junctions at more than one equally good position, so breakpoints within repeats remain approximate.

Discussion

Measuring somatic hypermutation has long meant choosing between reading molecules accurately and reading enough of them. This study shows that the choice is no longer necessary, provided the background is measured rather than assumed. SHMtrace takes whatever a platform delivers, reads or chromatograms, and replaces the two assumptions that make low-frequency calling from single reads unreliable for SHM. Instead of trusting quality scores, it measures the background in the AID-null libraries that SHM studies already include, at the level of substitution type, strand and base quality, and checks that calibration by holding out each control in turn. Instead of treating reads as replicates, it tests animals. Both replacements held: 81% of planted JH4 mutations and 90% on N114 VH were recovered at the false-discovery proportions the threshold targeted, while a quality-based caller either missed most true mutations or, made permissive, called as much background as the uncorrected rule, and pooling reads produced false positives in more than 90% of simulated experiments without a true difference.

The gap this fills is specific, and it sits between two mature bodies of software. Repertoire toolkits identify and summarise mutations well: IgBLAST assigns germlines32, Change-O and SHazaM quantify load22, SHMTool compares datasets21, and S5F models targeting23. They begin, however, from mismatches already taken to be real, which is why counting every mismatch to the germline separated AID from empty-vector N114 libraries by 2-fold where SHMtrace separated them by 188-fold. General-purpose variant callers do the opposite: they decide what is real, but from error models fitted to data of a different kind. LoFreq reads base qualities18; ClairS and DeepSomatic learn from tumour–normal pairs where the variant sits in a large share of reads24,25. Neither class measures what a particular SHM library does wrong. SHMtrace closes that circuit by making the AID-null control, already part of every well-designed SHM experiment, the source of the error model, and by carrying the measurement through to the animal-level comparison that the biology requires20. The held-out controls then set the threshold empirically, in the spirit of target–decoy analysis in proteomics29, rather than by assuming the distribution the p-values would need for a correction such as Benjamini–Hochberg30.

Seen this way, comparator performance is a matter of operating range rather than quality. LoFreq was designed for deep short-read data with calibrated qualities; its base-alignment correction suppressed nearly all nanopore calls here, and disabling it still limited recovery of low-support variants. That is not a defect on its intended data: a per-base error model fitted without matched controls has no way to reach this assay's library-specific background. The measurements say why. Above Q40, control error stopped decreasing and varied between runs (1–9 × 10−5; Figure S2), so the quality score runs out of information exactly where SHM calling needs it. ClairS and DeepSomatic recovered clean variants at higher allele fractions but few real SHM calls, and chemistry and model mismatches further qualify external-data comparisons. Clair3 and DeepVariant found none of the planted mutations, as expected of callers built to genotype a sample against a reference rather than to find a variant carried by a few reads. Geneious provided greater single-read recall on JH4 and Pim1 but more false calls on each amplicon. These are choices a study can make deliberately: sensitive mode raises the empirical target to 0.20 and recovers 72% of single-read mutations on JH4 and 87% on N114 VH at observed FDR 0.18 and 0.16, and events still unresolved individually are estimated in aggregate rather than discarded.

Because the background is measured rather than modelled, the same engine reads all four platforms, and what each contributes becomes a question about the experiment rather than the software. A Sanger clone is one molecule read once, so its mutations are read directly; there is no read support to weigh against a background, and so nothing for a per-call test to work on, and the AID-null clones measure a background reported beside the counts, with how much of each animal's total it explains. Nanopore and Illumina libraries carry matched AID-null arms, so each candidate is tested against the errors those controls make. Nanopore spans the whole amplicon, which made the 3′ decline visible without tiling, identified clones from the V(D)J junction and ordered their mutations into trees; Illumina gives depth without that linkage. PacBio combines length with per-base accuracy, and reading the donor, the join and the acceptor on one molecule recovered a known end-joining phenotype from a deposit with no AID-null arm, using junction shape in place of calibrated calls. Figure S21 sets out what each measured. Agreement between platforms is itself evidence: nanopore and Sanger reads from the same six mice gave substitution spectra correlated at r = 0.92 and the same AID-null contrast, so the cloning-free route reports the biology the classical assay reports.

Applying the same engine to other laboratories' data turned out to test it harder than any internal benchmark. Published sequence collections support reproducible reanalysis when clones are linked to animals and genotypes: SHMtrace recovered the principal JH4 frequency and G:C comparisons, matched SWHEL counts across five arms and twelve substitution types (2,804 of 2,809 tabulated mutations; r = 1.0000), and recovered 150 of 150 mutations an antibody-evolution study curated by hand, calling none its authors did not. Agreement was not complete, and the exceptions are as informative: Wt frequencies differed after denominator harmonisation, the published Mkln1 A:T decrease was not significant in the reanalysis, and the SWHEL total-mutation inference differed under a different test (Tables S4 and S5; Figures S14–S16). The Sanger re-analysis also surfaced bookkeeping problems, which SHMtrace now checks and reports rather than silently resolving. Deposits that include explicit clone-to-animal assignments and the original counting conventions, alongside sequences and chromatograms, would make such comparisons routine.

What this enables in practice is a short experiment-to-answer path that keeps its own evidence: read accounting, fixed-seed sampling and one engine behind the desktop and command-line interfaces, with every exclusion, threshold and flagged site written out for inspection. The power analysis sets the honest expectation. How many animals a study needs depends on the difference it seeks: under variation estimated from three Wt JH4 mice, the AID-competent against AID-null contrasts made here separate at three animals per group, whereas a two-fold difference between two AID-proficient groups would require approximately twenty, with considerable uncertainty in that estimate (Figure S8B). Sequencing depth cannot substitute for independent biological samples, and no analysis can recover a comparison the design did not support. Within that boundary the framework extends readily. It needs only a target, matched AID-null libraries and a sample sheet, which is why it transferred without retuning to off-target loci, switch regions, engineered diversification and base-editor substrates across twelve loci from eleven laboratories. Molecule-level consensus is the natural next addition: UMI collapsing of long reads already reduced technical error 57-fold39, and combining it with control-calibrated calling should push the measurable range below what single reads can resolve.

Limitations of the study

SHMtrace relies on representative AID-null controls prepared and sequenced alongside the samples. Error profiles can vary between runs and basecalling configurations, and calibration using controls from a different run was not evaluated. With one nanopore run per assay, the present results do not establish performance across independent runs.

The reported variants remain subject to technical uncertainty. Without UMIs, early PCR errors, recombination and repeated sequencing of PCR-derived molecules cannot be reliably distinguished from genuine mutations or expanded biological lineages. Mutation trees therefore describe relationships among reads rather than independently established cellular lineages. The default ≥50% allele-frequency filter can also remove genuine mutations shared by a dominant lineage. Excluded sites are reported for review, and experiments with a known starting sequence can use an optional reference policy that retains high-frequency differences. Single-read mutations that cannot be called individually are estimated only in aggregate, and unique-variant densities require comparison at matched sequencing depth.

The empirical false-discovery estimate depends on the available controls and can exceed its nominal target when background differs between libraries. Noisy-control exclusion requires at least three controls, and its effect should be reviewed. Sample-specific artifacts absent from the controls may still pass the test; spectrum and clustering flags can identify unusual patterns but require interpretation. At weakly mutated targets such as Pim1, signal remained close to background.

The core validation used three animals per group for JH4 and one locus per cell system. Settings were developed using these libraries and applied to public datasets without dataset-specific retuning, although the noisy-control exclusion rule was introduced after inspection of the Ramos results. Evaluation on an independent sequencing run with new biological samples would provide a stronger test of generalizability. Indel validation comprised nanopore spike-ins and one Illumina biological phenotype, while UMI validation used rRNA rather than SHM data. These experiments do not establish performance on biological nanopore indels or UMI-tagged SHM libraries; molecule-level consensus remains preferable when available. Two Sanger studies originated from the authors’ laboratory, while the independent Sanger study provided sequences without chromatograms or AID-null controls. Together, these datasets evaluate distinct parts of the workflow, with different limits on the conclusions each can support.

Method details

Germinal-center B cell isolation and DNA extraction

Peyer's patches were harvested post-mortem into cold FACS buffer ([FACS buffer composition, e.g. PBS with 2% fetal bovine serum]) and dissociated into single-cell suspensions by pressing them through a 70-µm strainer, then washed once. Cells were stained for 20–30 min on ice with anti-B220–FITC (RA3-6B2, 1:400), GL7–eFluor 660 (GL7, 1:100), and anti-Fas–BV421 (Jo2, 1:200), with anti-CD16/32 Fc block (clone 93, 1:100) and Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 506 (1:1,000) and washed twice. Germinal-center B cells (B220+GL7+Fas+) were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting on a BD FACSymphony S6 SE or FACSAria II, collecting approximately 10,000 cells per mouse; wild-type and Aicda−/− mice were processed on the same day with the same reagents, so that genotype is not confounded with handling. Genomic DNA was released from the sorted cells, or from 5 × 106 hybridoma cells, by proteinase K digestion (55 °C overnight, then 95 °C for 15 min; [lysis buffer and volume to confirm]), and lysate quality was checked by A260/280 and A260/230 ratios.

Amplification and sequencing

The N114 line carries a fixed, rearranged heavy-chain V region of the J558/IGHV1 family: IgBLAST assigns it to IGHV1-47-36 with IGHD1-1 and IGHJ3, and germline sequence of that V gene matches it over the whole V segment, so the assignment does not rest on this analysis. Relative to that germline the line carries two changes in CDR2, in frame and confirmed by the reads: the first replaces the germline glycine with an opal stop (TGA) and the second changes the following glycine to valine, giving W-I-Y-P-stop-N-V-N-T-K in place of W-I-Y-P-G-N-G-N-T-K. The stop is the point of the line: it prevents antibody production, and the original study scored its reversion as hypermutation46. Sequencing supersedes that readout: the analysis here measures mutation across the whole V region rather than reversion at one codon, and is made against the line's own reference throughout, which carries the stop; the V region is called N114 VH rather than by an allele. The 1,230-bp JH4 intron amplicon8,34 and the 655-bp N114 V-region amplicon46 were amplified with Platinum SuperFi II high-fidelity DNA polymerase. Each 50-µL reaction held 1× SuperFi II master mix, 0.5 µM of each primer and [lysate volume per PCR] of lysate, with a no-template control in every setup; wild-type and AID-null samples were amplified together. The JH4 primers were forward 5′-GGAATTCGCCTGACATCTGAGGACTCTGC-3′, reverse 5′-GACTAGTCCTCTCCAGTTTCGGCTGAATCC-3′, and the N114 primers forward 5′-TTACCTGGGTCTATGGCAGT-3′, reverse 5′-TGAAGGCTCAGAATCCCCC-3′. Cycling was 98 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 68.8 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; and a final 72 °C for 5 min. Products were checked on a 1% agarose gel for a single band of the expected size, then column-purified and submitted as separate, individually barcoded samples for full-length amplicon sequencing (Plasmidsaurus Premium PCR service), which uses ligation library preparation, R10.4.1 flow cells, Kit 14 chemistry, and super-accuracy basecalling (dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.2.0). Between 2,336 and 5,000 reads were delivered per library. For the Sanger benchmark, JH4 amplicons from separate PCRs of the same DNA had been cloned and sequenced45. Of the 447 chromatograms in that archive, which also covers other mice of that study, 184 were filed to the six mice sequenced here and re-analyzed; 134 returned analyzable clones (Figure S13).

Read preparation

SHMtrace reads FASTQ files, which may be gzipped or, for Illumina data, paired files that are merged on their overlap. Reads are then processed in the following order. First, reads longer than a locus-specific limit (2,000 bp for JH4 and 900 bp for N114 VH) are removed, as are reads with a mean expected error above 0.01 per base, calculated from Phred qualities28. Second, reads are aligned with minimap2 (map-ont preset, or sr for Illumina) against an index containing the target and any competing references, and each read is assigned to the sequence it aligns to best. Unmapped reads, reads assigned to a competing reference, reads with a supplementary alignment (chimeras), and reads aligned over less than a minimum length (800 bp for JH4 and 400 bp for N114 VH) are removed. For JH4, the competing references were a JH4-like paralog (the consensus of Agouti-type clones, approximately 12% divergent) and a recurrent contaminant haplotype. Because a full-length paralog read can align better to the longer target, reads carrying 10 or more bases specific to a competing reference, at sites where fewer than 20% of the sample's reads carry them, are also removed. Genuine JH4 reads carry 0–7 such bases, whereas paralog reads and chimeras carry 11–62. The fate of every read is recorded. Usable reads were then downsampled at random with a fixed seed (Python random, seed 0) to the analysis depth: 500 reads for JH4 and N114 VH and 2,000 reads for the IRCM loci. The analysis windows were positions 101–629 (529 bp, the window shared with Sanger) or 90–1,230 (full length) of the JH4 reference and positions 21–635 of the N114 VH reference. For loci whose window is not set by a preset, it runs from position 21 to 20 bases before the reference's end: Sμ 21–631 (611 bp), Pim1 21–882 (862 bp), off-target 1 21–988 (968 bp) and off-target 2 21–962 (942 bp).

Control-calibrated variant calling

Each observed single-nucleotide difference within the window is a candidate, including substitutions supported by one read. Control error rates are estimated by substitution type, strand and quality threshold, combining a pooled rate with position-specific counts. For a candidate with k supporting reads ranked q1 ≥ q2 ≥ …, the test evaluates, for each j ≤ k, the probability of at least j errors with quality ≥qj, using strand-specific depth and control rates multiplied by margin c. The reported p value is the smallest tail probability multiplied by k.

The margin c is chosen by leaving one control out at a time. Each control is tested against an error model built from the other controls, and c is the smallest value in the grid 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, and 50 at which no more than 1% of candidate tests pooled across held-out controls reach p < 0.01. In the analyses reported here, c was 5× on JH4, 10× on the full length, and 10× on N114 VH. The same held-out tests, made at the samples' analysis depth, are then used to set the significance threshold. Each held-out control is kept as a decoy, defined by its sorted p values and the number of bases it tested. For a sample and a candidate threshold t, the expected number of false calls is the mean, across decoys, of the number of decoy p values ≤ t, scaled by the ratio of the sample's tested bases to the decoy's when that ratio exceeds 1. Decoys are never scaled down to a shallower library, whose coarser p values they would otherwise underestimate. SHMtrace keeps the largest t at which the expected number of false calls is at most 0.05 times the number of sample p values ≤ t, an empirical false-discovery estimate inspired by target–decoy analysis29. A control is never used as its own decoy: when a control is tested, only the other controls serve as decoys. Each sample is judged against the controls of its own sequencing run when that run has at least two (one, for a control), and against all controls otherwise, with the report naming any sample whose run has no control; every JH4 and N114 VH sample shared its controls' run, while the public deposits do not record runs, so their samples were judged against all of their study's controls. A control whose held-out candidates at p ≤ 0.01 per base exceed five times the median of the other controls, and number at least five, is left out of the decoys (unless no decoy would remain) and named in the report; it still contributes to the error model, and is never removed from the analysis. The comparison needs a majority to judge against, so it is made only when at least two other controls remain, that is with three or more controls; with exactly two, the more variable library is named in the report but not left out, because which of the two is unusual cannot be established. The rule was written for one public dataset, the Ramos clones of Figure 7D, in which a single Aicda−/− clone suppressed every call; it was then applied to the other datasets and was already fixed when the off-target libraries were analyzed, where it excluded no control; it excluded one in the mOrange analysis. It is therefore a rule derived from data rather than a pre-registered one, and it is reported here so that its effect can be judged. With fewer than two controls, or for indels, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure30 across positions and alternative alleles is used instead. The number of controls therefore selects between four modes, and the output records which was used in its multiple_testing field, so a reader never has to infer it. With no control the background is not measured at all: every candidate is judged from base qualities, the correction is Benjamini–Hochberg, indels are not called, and the run carries a note saying so. With one control that library supplies the error model, but the margin cannot be fitted by leaving a control out and the default of 10, measured on JH4, is used; a control cannot be its own decoy, so there is no decoy set and the correction is again Benjamini–Hochberg; the noisy-control rule cannot act, since it needs two others to compare against. With two each is the other's decoy, the margin is fitted but can reach the top of its grid, and a disagreement between the pair is reported without acting on it, because with two there is no majority to say which is wrong. With three or more the margin is fitted by leave-one-out, the decoys are the peers, and a far noisier control is set aside automatically. A single control is enough to run but not to calibrate: the model fitted to that one library can absorb the signal, so results from it should be reported as mismatch rates without an FDR claim. Benjamini–Hochberg is not avoided because the p values are discrete: it controls the false-discovery rate for valid discrete p values, conservatively. It is avoided because of how candidates arise. A position enters the test only when at least one read already differs from the reference there, so the tested set is chosen by the same data that supply the evidence, and the null distribution of a candidate's p value is conditioned on that selection rather than superuniform on (0, 1]. The control-based threshold applies the same candidate-selection rule to samples and held-out controls, so it measures the corresponding background empirically. This does not establish formal FDR control: the decoys are few, their fitted nulls differ from the sample null, and their exchangeability with samples is an assumption. Figure S24 tests selected consequences of these limitations, and Figure S4 compares the target with the realised proportion on planted truth: the default was met when pooled, and targets below it were not; Figure S5 varies the number and source of the controls. Because the threshold targets a proportion, a library with many true mutations admits more false calls in absolute terms: in simulated polyclonal lineages, 13.7 per library, in exchange for recalling 83% of single-read mutations. Table S7 compares the default with a target of 0.01 and with the noisiest control's decoy count in place of the mean. At 0.01, false calls in those lineages fell to 3.0 per library but single-read recall fell to 38%; on the planted JH4 controls, recall fell from 81% to 17% without a lower false-discovery proportion (0.061), whereas N114 VH kept 76% recall at 0.005. With three controls, the decoy counts are too coarse to resolve a 1% target reliably. In the JH4 and N114 VH projects, both stricter settings removed calls only from mutation-bearing libraries; the group differences remained 71–138-fold (p ≤ 0.0078). The default was kept. Alternative alleles carried by at least 50% of covering reads are removed as germline or parental differences; other alleles at the same position remain eligible. Without controls, recommended mode uses a Poisson approximation to read-quality error probabilities; the separate pbinom mode implements the exact Poisson-binomial calculation.

Per-sample summaries report G:C share, transition share, G:C substitutions at WRC/GYW and A:T substitutions at WA/TW, with the corresponding motif opportunities in the analysis window. These describe spectrum and context, not uniquely identifiable repair-pathway contributions: uracil excision, mismatch repair and replication over uracil produce overlapping outcomes. Repair interpretations in Ung−/−Msh2−/− cells and Ugi/UNG fibroblasts are therefore grounded in the experimental perturbations rather than inferred from spectra alone.

Testing the calling procedure as a whole

End-to-end assessment used partitions and planted mutations from three existing Aicda−/− JH4 libraries (crm/fdr_validation.py). Cases used KO1 reads. Two-control analyses used one partition each from KO2/KO3; three-control analyses used two KO2 partitions and one KO3 partition. Case/control sets shared no read records, but without UMIs this does not establish original-molecule independence. Partitions are technical resamples rather than independent biological controls. Each run_project analysis reselected its margin, threshold and exclusions. Conditions were unmodified background, one noisy control, case-only artifact and shared case/control artifact. Artifacts occupied six positions at nominal 6% support; shared artifacts used identical alternate alleles, and case artifacts avoided planted biological sites. Control number (two/three), depth (250/500/1,000) and mutation burden (none/approximately 400 true recovered calls) were varied. Exclusion-on/off analyses used identical inputs (Figure S24). These stress tests assess behavior conditional on existing libraries, not transfer to a new run.

Under unmodified all-null background, discoveries occurred in 1/25 exclusion-on and 0/25 exclusion-off resamples; with one noisy control, both were 0/25. Clopper–Pearson 95% upper bounds of 20% and 14% for the unmodified condition do not establish a 5% bound. Shared-artifact resamples yielded 0/5 discoveries in both settings, each with a 52% upper bound. Case-only artifacts yielded discoveries in 5/5 under both settings, averaging 6.0 false calls. With biological mutations and exclusion enabled, mean per-library FDP was 3.8%, 3.0%, 3.7% and 5.1% for unmodified, noisy-control, shared-artifact and case-only conditions, respectively (five resamples each). Per-library FDP, pooled false/(true+false), and the all-null probability of any discovery are reported separately. These small conditional experiments do not establish general error control.

The noisy control was excluded in 25/25 all-null rule-on resamples. With biological mutations, exclusion-on/off analyses averaged 396.0/394.2 true calls and 12.2/11.2 false calls across five paired resamples. At least three controls are required to assess an outlier against peers. When the peer median is zero, ≥5 held-out candidates at p≤0.01 trigger exclusion; an unmodified control was consequently excluded in 3/25 all-null resamples. Every exclusion is recorded and warrants review, including sensitivity to retaining that control.

The default mean-decoy estimator was compared with control_decoy_estimate="max", which requires the threshold to satisfy the noisiest retained decoy (crm/fdr_estimator.py). On unmodified mutation-bearing inputs, mean FDP was 3.8% versus 2.5%, with 403.0 versus 389.0 true calls. For case-only artifacts, FDP was 5.1% versus 4.1%. Five paired resamples per condition used identical inputs. This compares implemented estimators; it is neither independent validation nor proof that the maximum guarantees FDR control.

Reported runtimes are wall-clock seconds for one run_project call on an Apple M2 Pro (12 cores, 32 GB; macOS), from prepared input to written results. They include file reading, mappy alignment, calling and sample summaries, but exclude reference construction and external benchmark containers. Sanger projects contain a few hundred chromatograms; nanopore projects contain six libraries analyzed at 500–2,000 reads. Input recovery and curation, including reconstruction of clone assignments from historical projects, took substantially longer and are excluded from compute times.

The Geneious rule was reproduced as an uncorrected approximate p ≤ 0.05 per candidate, which matched Geneious exports site by site (Jaccard index 0.97–0.98 on Wt JH4).

What each mutation measure counts

Three measures are reported per library, and they answer different questions; group tests then use one value per animal. A unique variant is a position and substituted base counted once per library, however many reads carry it; unique means distinct within that library and does not imply an independent mutational origin, since one lineage's trunk mutation is one unique variant. Unique-variant density is D / B × 105, where D is the number of unique variants and B the base observations at callable positions (inside the analysis window and the end trims), counted exactly as the read depth summed over those positions, not the number of reference positions. Because B grows with depth while D saturates, this density falls as depth rises even at a constant mutation rate, so it is compared only between libraries analyzed at the same depth (Figure S10). Read-weighted mutation frequency is Σ mi / (N × L) × 103, where mi is the number of called sites read i carries at or above the configured minimum base quality (0 for these read-library analyses), N the reads analyzed and L the window length; a mutation shared by many reads is counted once in each of them, so an expanded clone raises it. This fixed-window frequency credits every read with the whole window; an observed-base frequency, which counts only the bases each read covers, is reported beside it and agrees when reads span the window, as they did in the JH4 and N114 VH projects to within 2.4%. Clone-weighted frequency groups reads by their V(D)J junction, keeps a mutation when at least half the reads of its clone carry it (any read of a single-read clone), and is Σ ck / (K × L) × 103 over the K clones, so each clone counts once whatever its size. For wild-type JH4 library Wt2 at 500 reads over the 529-bp window these give 221 unique variants over 263,886 base observations (83.8 × 10−5), 747 read-level mutations over 264,500 bases (2.82 × 10−3), and 207 clonal mutations over 24 clones (16.3 × 10−3); the clone-weighted value is the largest because one clone holds 81% of the reads. For Sanger clones, where each clone is one molecule read once, the frequency denominator is the clones analyzed times L, with L the 529-bp window; the published studies used their own full consensus length and counted every filed clone, which is the convention Table S4 reports separately.

Two implementation details of the error model. The rate used at a site is the pooled rate for that substitution, strand and quality over all window positions, used as a floor, with that position's own control count shrunk toward it by m = 500 pseudo-observations, so a position the controls show erring often keeps its high rate while a position that happened to show none does not look error-free. A substitution class the controls never showed is given half a pseudo-count over the control bases observed, which keeps the quality tail above zero rather than allowing a p value of exactly zero. Indels are called separately, because nanopore indel errors are systematic rather than quality-driven: each exact indel is tested against the rate of its class (insertion or deletion, length up to 4, homopolymer run up to 5), counted per read spanning each position of the analysis window and used as a floor, with that site's own control rate shrunk toward it and inflated by a margin fitted as the substitution margin is; candidates are corrected by Benjamini–Hochberg; indels are counted only at quality ≥ 30 at the inserted bases or the bases flanking a deletion, and those carried by fewer than three reads are not called. UMI consensus groups reads by their tags, allowing up to 3 edits per tag end, greedily from the most abundant tag and merging a neighbor only when it is at most half as abundant; groups of fewer than 3 reads are dropped, and each remaining group becomes one quality-weighted consensus read. The single-read estimate divides the excess of single-read substitutions at quality ≥ 35 over the controls' rate by the share of that sample's correctly read bases reaching that quality, so that the estimate does not depend on the cutoff; that it does not between Q30 and Q40 is the check reported for JH4.

Single-read estimate, clones, mutation trees, and quality checks

Single-read mutations not called individually are estimated in aggregate. SHMtrace counts Q≥35 single-read substitutions outside the accepted call set, subtracts the control expectation scaled to sample bases, and divides by the fraction of correctly read bases reaching that quality. Control events are counted only at positions and alleles where the sample could show a single-read event, so neither its per-site calls nor alleles it carries in two or more reads add to the background. The 95% interval inverts a profile likelihood in which the sample count is Poisson in signal plus background and the control count Poisson in background, scaled by the between-control overdispersion; it stays nonzero when neither sample nor controls show an event. In simulation it covered the true count in 92.5–98.8% of experiments with Poisson background, and in 87–90% when three heterogeneous controls carried several background events, because three controls estimate the overdispersion imprecisely. The combined density's interval holds the per-site calls fixed.

Two different things are called a clone in this field, and this paper keeps them apart. A Sanger clone is one bacterial colony, that is one PCR product carried by one transformant and sequenced once: a unit of sequencing, which says nothing about which B cell the molecule came from, and two Sanger clones of one animal may well come from the same B cell lineage. A B cell clone is a lineage, defined here by a shared V(D)J junction, which is what the clone-weighted frequency and the mutation trees use. The two coincide only by accident. Where the text says a measure counts each clone once, it means the B cell clone; the Sanger denominator is clones analyzed × 529 bp, where those clones are the sequencing units. Sanger amplicons of the JH4 intron do not span the junction, so B cell clones cannot be identified in them, and clone-weighted frequencies are reported only for the nanopore libraries.

Junction grouping uses the twenty bases immediately upstream of alignment starts occurring within 18 bp of JH4. Sequences are clustered greedily around abundance-ranked centroids with edit distance ≤3 and a free 5′ end. Each lineage's majority profile retains calls supported by at least half its reads. Tree nodes merge mutations supported by ≥2 lineage reads with Jaccard overlap ≥0.9; each node's parent is the smallest node containing ≥90% of its supporting reads. A read fits when its nodes occupy one ancestral path. The four-gamete test requires each of the four presence/absence combinations for a mutation pair in at least two reads.

The targeting index is mean S5F mutability23 at mutated positions divided by the window mean. A one-sided permutation test uses 2,000 random position sets; the model was exported from SHazaM 1.2.0. Positional-clustering flags identify 10-base windows with more calls than expected under a Poisson distribution, with Bonferroni correction across windows (p < 10−3). Figure S22 summarizes the workflow; Figure S23 illustrates the analytical rules using JH4 libraries. Sanger traces are assigned through a sample sheet and trimmed to their longest reliably aligned Q≥20 segment. All Q≥20 substitutions are counted after germline filtering. Quality-control flags identify copied files, unexpectedly shared clones between animals (Poisson test weighted by mutation frequency across animals), excess mutations in controls, label mismatches and mutation-load outliers. Flags are reported for review without automatic exclusion. These flags were tested on forty simulated projects, each carrying one instance of every bookkeeping error: a changed label was detectable only where the affected clones shared mutations with another animal's clones, because a label alone leaves no trace in the sequence, and no correctly assigned clone was flagged in error among the 6,018 tested (crm/sanger_validation_summary.json).

The Sanger route counts Q≥20 substitutions without per-call significance testing. AID-null clones provide the observed Q≥20 mismatch rate after their own germline differences are excluded, stratified by quality and position. Applying these rates to each sample's bases gives the expected background count, reported beside observations without subtraction. Sites mismatched in more than one control clone are annotated on corresponding sample calls. This background estimate contextualizes Sanger counts but does not determine acceptance of individual substitutions.

For the Maea Sanger study34, chromatograms from four 96-well plates and the pilot plate of cohort two were downloaded from the sequencing facility portal. Mouse assignments were reconstructed from the original project folders using screen recordings. Four traces filed under two mice were retained under both assignments. Published per-mouse values came from Source Data: Figure 2h for mutation frequencies, including Aicda−/− mice; Figure 2i for G:C transitions and transversions; and Figure 2g for clone distributions. Twelve-type counts were transcribed from Supplementary Figure 9b, using printed cell values where they conflicted with printed totals. Data S1 contains the sample sheet and transcribed tables.

For the Mkln1 study45, mouse assignments were reconstructed from original project folders using a screen recording. A separate folder contained 58 later-run Mkln1−/− traces named for the same three mice. These were included under their named mice because the published substitution counts exceed the earlier preprint by eight mutations, matching the number and types of SHMtrace calls in this batch. The files do not establish which later clones entered the published tables or how the three knockout folders map to published mice #1–#3; comparisons therefore use groups rather than matched mice. Each later clone carries at most two mutations and cannot be placed using the matched nanopore libraries, because assignment requires a read carrying at least three of its mutations.

A sensitivity analysis used an evidence-based Mkln1 clone set with rules fixed before analysis and independent of published values. Paralog amplicons and trace copies were set aside as in the primary analysis. Lineages filed under two mice, identified by duplicate-clone and shared-mutation checks, were sought in matched JH4 nanopore libraries after excluding positions mismatched in >0.5% of Aicda−/− reads. A read supported a lineage when it carried more than half its shared mutations and at least three. Clones without an original mouse assignment remained excluded, as in the primary analysis.

For the SWHEL study35, all 1,795 GenBank clones (MH198782–MH200576) were downloaded and treated as Sanger sequences; the sample sheet records each accession, host and well. GenBank provides no base qualities, so Q30 was assigned as a processing convention rather than measured quality. The majority-consensus reference converged in two iterations. The analysis window covered the interval represented in ≥99% of clones (558 bp of the 576-bp consensus). Host and genotype assignments followed deposited records, with the UGI group kept separate. No Aicda−/− group was available for background estimation. Published per-group counts were transcribed from the source study's Table 1, which counts each sequence independently. The Wt deposit names twelve hosts versus thirteen reported, and AT40 contains 88 clones, approximately twice the other collections, suggesting that two animals may share this label. Pooled counts are unaffected. Animal-level tests use deposited host names and were repeated without AT40; clones were not arbitrarily split between inferred animals. Excluding AT40 gave p = 1.6 × 10−10, 8.5 × 10−6, 5.0 × 10−10, and 0.18 for the four substitution classes and 2.6 × 10−4 for total mutation, versus 9.7 × 10−11, 7.7 × 10−6, 1.2 × 10−10, 0.14 and 3.8 × 10−4 with all hosts.

The high-allele-fraction rule removes any change carried by at least 50% of the reads covering it, as a germline or parental difference. Because a mutation shared by a dominant lineage reaches the same frequency, we measured what the rule costs by simulating lineages whose dominant clone is itself mutated. Libraries were built on the JH4 AID-null reads as for Figure 3E, with the dominant clone holding 40%, 60% or 80% of the reads and carrying its own trunk mutations (mean 6, as the other clones), 3 libraries per abundance, each planted library called with the other two controls as its null. A planted mutation was counted as exposed to the rule when the reads carrying it reached the threshold at its position. At 40% none did. At 60% and 80%, 0–4 and 0–4 per library did, and none of them was called: a mutation at or above the threshold is removed before any test. Across libraries the read-weighted frequency fell to as much as 40% and 62% below the truth and the clone-weighted frequency to 26% and 34%, while libraries in which nothing crossed the threshold were unaffected. Each sample's report now lists every site the rule removed with its read count, depth and frequency, so a library with a large mutated clone can be recognized and its sites inspected.

Benchmark against other callers

Figure 4 and Table S1 are built by crm/benchmark_real.py, which drives the calling stage directly so that every caller sees exactly the same reads; the product itself runs run_project. Because those are two code paths, they were compared library by library (crm/route_check.py). On the JH4 and N114 VH libraries of this study the two agree exactly, all twelve giving identical calls. On the four public nanopore datasets they do not, because the leave-one-control-out margin is fitted over a different set: the benchmark route fits it from the two peers of each held-out control, run_project across all three together, so the thresholds differ. Counts then move in both directions, for instance 88 against 101 calls in one Sμ wild-type library and 6 against 1 in one Pim1 wild type. The separations are unchanged in direction and in order of magnitude under either route (JH4 141 against 141, N114 VH 85 against 84, Sμ LPS 66 against 84, Sμ CH12 94 against 171, off-target 1 53 against 46, off-target 2 28 against 24), and Pim1 stays below separation either way, at 1.8 against 0.8. Figure 4 therefore reports the benchmark route throughout, for comparability between callers, and Table S3 reports run_project, where the two coincide.

For each library, the reads analyzed by SHMtrace were written out, aligned with minimap2 using the same preset with secondary alignments turned off, and sorted and indexed with SAMtools. They were then called with LoFreq 2.1.5 call, restricted to the analysis window, at three settings: default settings (base-alignment quality on, Bonferroni correction, default filters); -B (base-alignment quality off); and -B -a 0.05 -b 1 --no-default-filter (uncorrected p ≤ 0.05, filters off). Substitutions at an allele frequency below 0.5 were kept, matching SHMtrace's germline filter, and all callers used the same density denominator for each library. In the spike-in tests, 60 sites per replicate were chosen with WRC/GYW motifs weighted 4-fold and WA/TW motifs 2-fold, and each substitution was written into 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, or 20 reads covering that site while keeping each read's own base quality. Three replicates were performed for each held-out control. For Figure S4 the same libraries were called at target false-discovery rates of 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20 and 0.30 (fdr_alpha), every other setting at its default; the realised proportion is false calls over all calls, pooled over a locus's libraries. For Figure S5 (crm/controls_design.py), the validation libraries of Figure S24 (KO1 carrying planted polyclonal lineages, or none, against halves of KO2 and KO3) were run through run_project with one to four controls, and with one control carrying recurrent artifacts, 10 repeats per setting; the third and fourth controls are halves of the same two animals. Each Sμ LPS AID-null library, with 60 planted substitutions and three draws, was called against its experiment's other two controls, two or three CH12 AID-null controls, or all five. The SRA reads carry no run identifiers, so this compares experiments, not verified sequencing runs. For the dilution series (crm/titration.py), 500 usable reads were drawn with a fraction f from an AID-active library and the rest from one AID-null library (JH4 KO1; N114 D1), 3 draws per fraction, and called by run_project against the other two AID-null libraries; expected mutation frequency is f times the donor at f = 1 plus (1 − f) times the diluent. N114 VH used ancestral_reference, because its reference is the parental construct: with the default 50% rule, mutations fixed within a subclone are removed at f = 1 but counted once diluted, and D3 then read 1.84 per 103 bp at f = 0.5 and 0.63 at f = 1. For Figure S12 (crm/immcantation_compare.py), the 500 reads SHMtrace analyzed from each N114 VH library were passed to IgBLAST 1.22.0 (AIRR output) against the IMGT mouse IGH germlines, or with the parental V segment (294 bp) as the only V germline, with and without bases below Q20 replaced by N first (the rule of pRESTO's quality masking). SHazaM observedMutations counted substitutions between v_sequence_alignment and v_germline_alignment; a library's value is the mean over reads. SHMtrace's value is the supporting reads of its calls in the same segment over analyzed reads times its length. MiXCR was not run. The IRCM runs (PRJNA1481264) were downloaded with the SRA Toolkit, validated with vdb-validate, and subsampled to 6,000 reads (seed 0). The reference for each locus was the majority consensus of its first Aicda−/− library, built with minimap2 and SAMtools consensus and iterated to convergence. The two off-targets of the same BioProject were added later and analyzed the same way, at 2,000 reads, with references built by the same consensus procedure (1,008 and 982 bp, converged in two iterations). Other loci of that BioProject were set aside: Cd83 and Ly6e yielded no calls in any library, including at 30,000 reads, and the remaining cell contexts provide two libraries per group or a single Aicda−/− library, which is below what the control-based test and the animal-level comparison need.

Two somatic callers that compare a case library with a matched normal, ClairS 0.5.124 and DeepSomatic 1.9.025, and two germline callers, Clair3 2.0.326 and DeepVariant 1.9.027, were run from their official Docker images on the same planted mutations, using the same alignments as LoFreq and restricted to the analysis window. For the somatic callers, the normal was the pooled usable reads of the other two AID-null libraries, the libraries from which SHMtrace's null is fitted. Each caller was run at its defaults for R10.4.1 nanopore reads (ClairS ont_r10_dorado_sup_5khz; Clair3 r1041_e82_400bps_sup_v520; DeepVariant ONT_R104; DeepSomatic ONT) and at the lowest allele fraction it accepts (ClairS --snv_min_af 0.005 --min_coverage 4; Clair3 --snp_min_af 0.01 --min_coverage 4; DeepVariant and DeepSomatic candidate thresholds of 1% and 0.5% of reads, with two supporting reads). PRJNA1481264 reports PromethION sequencing without the chemistry, so the same R10.4.1 models were used for Sμ and Pim1; those models assume a flow-cell and basecaller combination those libraries may not match, and a mismatch would count against the caller for reasons unrelated to SHM. This qualifies the somatic and germline callers on the public reads as LoFreq's design qualifies it on nanopore reads: comparators run outside the conditions they were built for, not evidence of general superiority either way. Substitutions with FILTER PASS below 0.5 allele fraction were kept, as for the other callers. To find the allele fraction at which the somatic callers begin to work, 30 substitutions were also planted into 25, 50, and 100 of 500 reads (5%, 10%, and 20%) of each held-out JH4 control, which otherwise carries few mutations. ClairS and DeepSomatic were also run on the real AID-active libraries: the wild-type JH4 libraries against the pooled Aicda−/− reads, and the pMX-Aicda N114 VH subclones against the pooled empty-vector reads (500 reads each, seed 0).

Simulations

Lineage simulations partitioned one null library into approximately forty power-law-sized clones. Each received a random 20-bp junction, J trimming from −3 to +12 bp, and S5F-weighted trunk, branch, subbranch and private mutations with gamma-distributed loads. In the expanded-clone scenario, one unmutated clone supplied 60% of reads. Each control was held out against the other two. Quantification was assessed at 125, 250, 500 and 1,000 reads on JH4/N114 VH (168 libraries). Group simulations used log-normal animal rates (coefficient of variation 1), negative-binomial counts and 262,913 base observations per animal, across 300 experiments per condition. Forty Sanger projects included realistic trace qualities, unusable traces and one of each planted tracking error.

Public PacBio class-switch junctions

PRJNA83166644 holds Sμ–Sγ1 switch products from wild-type, Shld1−/−, Xlf−/− and Shld1−/−Xlf−/− primary B cells, three mice per genotype, one PacBio run per mouse. Although the archive names the files as subreads, the reads are of high-accuracy quality: aligned to the bundled Sμ fragment over 600 bp or more, their median identity is 99.8%, so the map-hifi preset was used in place of the module's map-ont default. Donor and acceptor references were cut from the GRCm39 IgH locus (chromosome 12, minus-strand orientation): Sμ with the forward primer site, chr12:113,386,000–113,392,500, and Sγ1 through Cγ1 to the reverse primer site, chr12:113,290,000–113,304,000, the windows being fixed by where the reads themselves start and stop (crm/build_switch_reference.py, which refuses to write unless the project's own JH4 reference and the bundled Sμ fragment both align inside the fetched locus). Junctions were called with shmtrace.switch (crm/switch_pacbio.py): reads were oriented to the donor, the donor and acceptor parts taken as the two best forward alignments, and junctions whose breakpoints agreed within 3 bp on both sides counted once as a unique junction. Each library also carries a large population of short off-target molecules, median about 350 bp against about 1.3 kb for the reads that yield junctions, so read accounting is reported per sample. Genotypes were compared with one value per mouse, by Welch test and by permutation; with three mice per genotype the permutation p cannot fall below 0.1, so both are reported. The fifteen comparisons there, five features against three genotypes, are reported unadjusted, with each group mean beside its p value so the size of the difference is visible and not only its significance. Adjusting them together by Benjamini–Hochberg changes nothing: nine reach 0.05 before and nine after. The engineered-diversification contrast of Figure S18A is a single pre-specified comparison, AID against APOBEC3A in each genotype, so no adjustment arises there. Acceptor-breakpoint ambiguity was measured directly, by re-aligning each junction's first 60 acceptor bases on their own with the short-read preset and counting placements within five matched bases of the best, because the module's own flag depends on secondary alignments that minimap2 does not report for these reads. What that ambiguity costs the comparisons was then measured rather than assumed (crm/switch_ambiguity_sensitivity.py): dropping every junction whose acceptor segment has more than one near-equal placement removes 2–21% of a sample's junctions, and 13 of the 15 genotype comparisons stay on the same side of 0.05. Two do not, in opposite directions: microhomology of four bases or more in Shld1−/− strengthens from p = 0.065 to 0.005, and insertions in Xlf−/− weaken from p = 0.016 to 0.060. Those two should be read as provisional.

Public engineered-diversification datasets

Four datasets in which a deaminase is directed deliberately were analysed against the construct each experiment began with, which is what ancestral_reference is for: there the reference is a known ancestor, so a change that selection drove high is the result and not germline. GSE7778843 comprises Sμ libraries from Wt/Aicda−/− primary B cells under DMSO, idelalisib or duvelisib, three replicates per genotype/treatment. Quantification and statistical analysis

Groups were compared with one value per library, each library being one animal, subclone, clone, culture or experiment as Table S2 states; reads were never treated as replicates. The Welch test on log10 rates is the primary test throughout, chosen before the analyses reported here; the negative-binomial and permutation tests are reported alongside it as sensitivity analyses, and Figure 3H compares all three. The negative-binomial test models each library's mutation count k with mean n·r, where n is that library's bases and r its group's rate, and variance n·r + φ(n·r)2 with one dispersion φ shared by both groups and fitted by maximum likelihood under the full model. No moderation or shrinkage across features is applied, since there is one endpoint. The statistic is the likelihood ratio against a common rate, and its null distribution comes from 499 parametric bootstrap samples drawn under that common rate with the fitted φ, because two to ten libraries per group are too few for the asymptotic χ2. A group with no calls keeps rate zero rather than being floored. The permutation test is exact when the number of allocations allows, which sets a floor on the p value it can return: with three libraries per group there are only 20 allocations, so the smallest two-sided p is 0.10 and the test cannot reach 0.05 whatever the difference. Its apparent power of zero at n = 3 in Figure 3H is therefore arithmetic, not a property of the data, and it is why the Welch test is primary at these group sizes. Each cell of Figure 3H is 300 simulated experiments, so a reported share carries a Monte Carlo 95% interval no wider than ±5.6 percentage points (Wilson); under the null at n = 3 the Welch test rejected in 3.7% (95% CI 2.1–6.4) and the negative-binomial test in 5.0% (3.1–8.1), while pooling reads rejected in 91.3% (87.6–94.0). The Welch test uses log10 rates, with libraries without calls placed at their one-event density; a fold against such libraries therefore depends on that convention and is not a confidence bound. The same rule, one event on the library's own exposure, is used for every log-scale test, fold and plotted geometric mean; the software also reports each test with half an event and with a third of the smallest observed rate, as labelled sensitivity analyses, never as the primary result. The permutation test is exact. Folds are ratios of geometric means. Spectra were compared by pooled Pearson correlation across the 12 substitution types with a Fisher interval, by a repeated-measures test of the platform-by-substitution-type interaction with mouse as the matching factor, and by paired Šídák-adjusted t tests. Sample sizes, statistical tests, and dispersion measures are given in each figure legend. Analyses used Python 3.13 with NumPy and SciPy50.

Figure S22. SHMtrace analysis workflow, related to STAR Methods. Sequence files are assigned to biological replicates through a sample sheet, filtered and aligned competitively against target, paralog and contaminant references. Matched AID-null libraries provide the background model for read-library substitution tests; held-out controls serve as decoys for threshold selection. Sanger substitutions are counted separately, with measured background reported alongside the counts (Figure 1C). Length limits are locus-specific (STAR Methods). The default ≥50% allele filter excludes likely germline differences but can also remove acquired mutations in dominant clones. Group comparisons use one value per animal. Illustrative panels show the error-rate grid by substitution and quality, expected versus observed candidate support, and calls versus decoys across thresholds. Outputs include density, mutation frequency, base-composition-corrected 12-type spectra, WRC/GYW and WA/TW targeting, positional profiles, clones, mutation trees and indels; Sanger outputs also report expected background counts. Bookkeeping flags do not automatically remove traces. A trace deliberately assigned to two animals is retained in both, and suspected accidental copies are flagged for review. Only rows explicitly marked as excluded in the sample sheet are omitted, with reasons recorded. Sample-sheet rows are illustrative. Figure S23 details the analytical rules and equations.
