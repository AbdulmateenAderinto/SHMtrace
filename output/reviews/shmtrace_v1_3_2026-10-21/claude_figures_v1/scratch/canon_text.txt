## INTRO
Somatic hypermutation (SHM) diversifies immunoglobulin (Ig) genes in germinal-center B cells. It is initiated by Activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) which deaminates cytosines in Ig DNA. Replication over the resulting uracils, together with uracil excision and mismatch repair, generates substitutions at G:C and A:T base pairs[@muramatsu2000,@dinoia2007,@methot2017]. Because each repair pathway leaves its own footprint, the frequency, position and spectrum of mutations are therefore key readouts of AID activity and broader lesion processing[@dinoia2002,@rada2004,@rada2002,@zeng2001]. Relatedly, the mouse J_{H}4 downstream intron is widely used as a genomic reporter because it undergoes SHM without directly contributing to antigen binding[@jolly1997,@lebecque1990]. AID activity read out from J_{H}4 amplicons can be measured on various sequencing platforms, each reporting a different kind of evidence. Cloned-amplicon Sanger sequencing reads one molecule at a time at high per-base confidence, though it yields only tens of sequences per animal[@sible2021]. Short reads typical of Illumina sequencers give depth but may not span the complete region[@martin2023]. Long reads from ONT and PacBio span the J_{H}4, so mutations stay linked across the amplicon, and nanopore accuracy now reaches modal values above 99% with R10.4.1 flow cells[@zhang2023,@sereika2022,@sanderson2024]; nanopore sequencing has also been used to sequence Ig genes, where consensus sequencing can absorb errors[@minervini2021,@subassatish2022]. What all platforms, however, share is the need to separate AID-driven mutation from their own error. A wild-type J_{H}4 intron carries roughly one mutation per 100 bases, whereas the residual error of a single filtered read, especially with nanopore, is within one or two orders of magnitude of that rate and is concentrated at particular positions and sequence contexts[@delahaye2021]. Two common analysis practices make this problem worse. First, general-purpose low-frequency variant callers judge each base by its own quality score[@wilm2012], and the simple rules used in commercial software test candidates one at a time without correcting for the number of tests[@kearse2012]. In the configurations evaluated here, neither measures the errors a particular library makes, so mismatches that recur at one position, from PCR, sequence context or a co-amplified paralog, can be counted as SHM mutations. Second, treating reads from the same animal as independent replicates overstates statistical certainty because multiple reads can derive from the same B-cell clone or PCR-amplified molecule[@lazic2010]. Matched AID-null libraries address technical background, whereas group comparisons must account for biological replication by using one summary value per animal. Existing SHM software summarises previously identified mutations[@maccarthy2009,@gupta2015,@yaari2013] but does not use matched AID-null controls to distinguish candidate variants from technical background. Here we describe SHMtrace, which integrates variant calling calibrated against matched AID-null controls from the same sequencing run with mutation profiling and group comparisons across independent biological replicates, starting from delivered reads or Sanger chromatograms. We validated it with planted mutations, simulated lineages, twelve loci from eleven laboratories across four platforms, Sanger sequencing of the same mice, and deposited clones from three published Sanger studies, and compared it against LoFreq[@wilm2012], the long-read somatic callers ClairS[@zheng2026] and DeepSomatic[@park2026], the germline callers Clair3[@zheng2022] and DeepVariant[@poplin2018], and the uncorrected Geneious test[@kearse2012].

## RESULTS
h SHMtrace calibrates substitutions against AID-null controls p SHMtrace starts from the files that a sequencing provider or core facility returns (Figures 1 and S1). Nanopore and Illumina reads are first filtered by length and expected error[@edgar2015] and aligned with minimap2^{29} against the target together with competing references. Reads that align better to a paralog, are chimeric, or carry ten or more paralog-specific bases are removed (Figure 1B), and the same number of usable reads is then drawn at random from every library. Sanger chromatograms are assigned to animals through a sample sheet and trimmed to their high-quality core, and reads tagged with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) are collapsed to one consensus per molecule. p The central step is the variant test (Figures 1A and 1C). For each substitution type, strand, and base quality, SHMtrace measures how often the AID-null libraries of the same run show that change, and asks whether a candidate is supported by more reads, at the qualities observed, than this background explains. Because quality scores overstate certainty at the highest qualities (Figure S2), the control rates are multiplied by a margin chosen by leaving one control out at a time (STAR Methods). The held-out controls then serve as decoys, in an approach inspired by target–decoy analysis[@elias2007]: SHMtrace keeps the most permissive cutoff at which estimated false calls are at most 5% of calls. This is an empirical estimate targeting 5%, not a guarantee: it relies on a few controls representing the sample's background, and the same controls also choose the margin. Candidates arise only where a read already differs, so the set tested is data-dependent, and its null p-values need not be superuniform, as Benjamini–Hochberg[@benjamini1995] requires; SHMtrace counts false calls in controls selected the same way. Alternative alleles carried by at least half the reads at a position are set aside as likely germline or parental differences. This is a heuristic, not a test: a mutation shared by a lineage that dominates a library crosses the same threshold, so every site it removes is reported with its read count and frequency. p The read-library test can retain a candidate supported by a single read; repeated sequencing of the same original molecule is not required. SHMtrace uses a separate Sanger counting route (Figure 1C): every substitution at base quality ≥ 20 is counted, and the AID-null clones' mismatch rate gives the expected background count, reported separately without subtraction. The germline filter, window, automatic checks and animal-level comparisons are shared. The Sanger results below therefore test trace reading, counting, bookkeeping and group comparisons, not the control-calibrated test. p Two C-to-T candidates in an external wild-type Sμ library illustrate how matched controls distinguish candidate mutations from recurrent background (WT3; Figure 2). The substitution at position 165, within a WRC hotspot (TACCT), was supported by 20 of 2,000 reads, compared with 15 of 1,941 reads at position 97 (AGCTT). Both had support on both strands and similar median base qualities (Q34 and Q33, respectively). However, no control supported the position-165 substitution at Q≥20, whereas KO1 carried the position-97 substitution in 14 of 1,879 reads at a median quality of Q33. SHMtrace retained the position-165 candidate (p = 5 × 10^{−19}) and rejected the recurrent position-97 candidate (p = 1), which the Geneious rule called in two controls and all three wild-type libraries. Across WT3, 743 distinct substitutions were evaluated as potential mutations, of which 88 met the control-calibrated calling threshold (p ≤ 0.009; Figure 2D). The remaining candidates could not be distinguished confidently from background. p SHMtrace compares groups using one summary value per biological replicate, with Welch tests on log-transformed rates, negative-binomial tests with parametric bootstrapping, and permutation tests (Figure 1D; Figure S3 shows each output with an example). It characterizes mutation patterns through substitution spectra, positional clustering, hotspot use and an S5F-based targeting index[@yaari2013]. Additional analyses estimate single-read mutations that could not be called individually, group reads into clones by their junction sequences, reconstruct read-level mutation trees and estimate statistical power. The command-line and desktop interfaces run the same analysis engine. h Planted mutations and simulated lineages quantify recall and false calls p We planted 60 AID-weighted substitutions into each held-out control library, preserving base qualities and assigning 1–20 supporting reads per site, with three repetitions per control (Figure 3). On J_{H}4, SHMtrace recalled 81% overall, and 95% of mutations were supported by at least three reads, with 2.6 false calls per library (observed FDR 0.050). In contrast, Geneious recalled more single-read mutations (60% versus 31%) but produced 4.9 false calls per library (FDR 0.083). Default LoFreq recalled none; disabling its short-read-oriented base-alignment quality correction yielded 34% recall, restricted to mutations with at least five supporting reads. Permissive settings increased recall to 56% with 4.4 false calls per library (Figures 3A and 3D). p ClairS[@zheng2026] and DeepSomatic[@park2026] were supplied with the other AID-null libraries as matched normals. Neither recovered planted mutations at default settings. Across 2,160 substitutions on J_{H}4, N114 V_{H}, Sμ and *Pim1*, permissive ClairS recovered none; permissive DeepSomatic recalled 19%, 18%, 5% and 3%, respectively, with at most 0.4 false calls per library. Recall increased at higher allele fractions, approaching complete recovery at 20% on J_{H}4 (Figure 3C). On real J_{H}4 libraries, ClairS reported one mutation and DeepSomatic at most 13 across three mice, compared with 221, 344 and 73 SHMtrace calls. Corresponding N114 V_{H} totals were one and at most 14, compared with 70, 49 and 173. ClairS rejected both Wt3 candidates supported by at least 20% of reads. Most SHMtrace-retained calls had much lower fractions: among 1,197 calls across four loci, 53% occurred in <1% of reads, 93% in <5% and 99.5% in <20% (Figure 2E). Germline callers Clair3 and DeepVariant recovered none of the 2,160 planted substitutions, consistent with their intended operating range (Table S1). p On N114 V_{H}, SHMtrace recalled 90% of planted substitutions, including 58% supported by one read, with 2.6 false calls per library (FDR 0.045; Figure 3B). Geneious recalled 88% with 2.8 false calls (FDR 0.050); LoFreq without base-alignment quality recalled 34%. On external Sμ and *Pim1* controls at 2,000 reads, SHMtrace recalled 63% and 72% at FDR 0.055 and 0.035. Geneious recalled 59% and 64% at FDR 0.114 and 0.050; LoFreq recalled 0–26%, with permissive FDR 0.27–0.52 (Figure 3D). p Calling the same libraries at targets from 1% to 30% tested whether the threshold delivers the rate it targets. At the default 5%, the pooled false-discovery proportion was 3.5–5.5% across the four loci, and looser targets stayed at or below their stated rate except J_{H}4 at 10% (13.8%) (Figure S4A). Targets below 5% did not deliver their stated rate on J_{H}4, Sμ or *Pim1* (2.0–6.1% at a 1% target; N114 V_{H}, 0.5%): false calls per library levelled off while recall fell, from 81% to 17% on J_{H}4 (Figures S4B and S4C). p Varying the controls through the whole procedure on J_{H}4 showed how many are needed (Figure S5). One control leaves no decoys, so the threshold falls back to Benjamini–Hochberg: it recalled 68% of planted lineage mutations, against 93% with two controls and 95% with three or four, at false-discovery proportions of 2.8–3.1%. All-null libraries yielded no calls with any number of controls (40 runs). One control carrying recurrent artifacts changed false calls little, with or without the noisy-control rule. On Sμ, AID-null controls from a second experiment performed like the matched controls (66–68% versus 63% recall), and pooling all five lowered false calls from 2.2 to 0.8 per library. p Diluting real hypermutated reads into AID-null reads tested the estimates on mutations AID made, with their clonality and clustering (Figure S6). On N114 V_{H}, mutation frequency was 0.87–0.97 of the expected value at 25–50% donor reads, and calls were made from 1–5% donor reads. On J_{H}4, detection was all-or-nothing and depended on the donor: calls began at 2% donor reads for Wt3 and at 50% for Wt2, and no call was made below that. p Indel validation used ten planted insertions or deletions of 1–40 bp per library, supported by 1–20 reads, with eight draws per control and error models fitted to the other controls. Among events supported by at least three reads, recall was 72% on J_{H}4 and 91% on N114 V_{H}, with 0.33 and zero false calls per library. Events supported by one or two reads were excluded by the three-read requirement. J_{H}4 single-base homopolymer deletions were least reliably recovered (19%); recall was 100% for 2-bp insertions and 89% for deletions ≥10 bp (Figure S7). p We next simulated approximately 40 B-cell clones per J_{H}4 library, with clone sizes following a power-law distribution. Mutations were introduced at positions sampled according to S5F mutability, including mutations shared across an entire clone, restricted to individual branches, or unique to individual sequences (Figure 3E). SHMtrace recalled 83% of single-read mutations versus 57% for Geneious, with FDR 0.037 versus 0.012 and 13.7 versus 4.0 false calls per library. In null libraries, false calls averaged 0.7 versus 4.3. Junction clustering recovered clone assignments with adjusted Rand indices[@hubert1985] of 0.94–0.98. When an unmutated clone contributed 60% of reads, read-weighted frequency was 6.2 versus a true 6.0 per 10^{3} bp, while the majority-rule clone-weighted measure recovered 94% of its corresponding true burden (14.6 versus 15.5; Figure 3F). p The dominant unmutated clone could not trigger the ≥50% allele filter. Repeating the simulation with a mutated dominant clone showed no loss at 40% abundance, but all mutations crossing the threshold at 60% or 80% were removed. Read-weighted and clone-weighted estimates fell by as much as 62% and 34%, respectively (STAR Methods). Excluded sites are reported for inspection. p At 500 reads per library, read-weighted mutation frequencies were within 2% of the simulated values for both J_{H}4 and N114 V_{H} (Figure 3G). Unique-variant density was less consistently recovered: J_{H}4 estimates were 97% of the true density at 500 reads and 94% at 1,000 reads, whereas N114 V_{H} reached 77% at 1,000 reads. This endpoint counts each distinct substitution once and divides by the number of analyzed bases. As sequencing depth increases, additional reads can carry mutations already counted, increasing the denominator without adding new variants; depth also affects detection of rare mutations. Including the aggregate estimate of single-read mutations that were not called individually improved recovery to 96–100% of the true density across the simulated scenarios. In a separate test, planting 25, 50 or 100 single-read mutations increased this estimate by an average of 95% of the number introduced (Figure S8A). Comparisons of unique-variant density therefore require matched sequencing depth. p Group-test calibration was assessed in 300 simulations per condition, with between-animal coefficient of variation 1 (Figure 3H). Under no true difference, pooled-read tests returned p < 0.05 in 91% of 3-versus-3 and 94% of 5-versus-5 experiments. Negative-binomial rejection rates were 5.0% and 5.7%. With three animals per group, that test detected two-fold and four-fold changes in 12% and 30% of simulations, illustrating the limited power at this sample size. h Matched controls set the background in real libraries p We next compared callers on real libraries with matched AID-null controls (Table S2; Figure 4). This adjustment increases the expected error probability used in the test, so a candidate requires stronger evidence to be called; it does not change the observed mutation count. The factor is selected by testing each control against the remaining controls and choosing the smallest tested multiplier for which no more than 1% of held-out candidate tests yield p < 0.01. Without adjustment, 5.4% of J_{H}4 and 9.6% of N114 V_{H} held-out tests fell below this threshold. Factors of five for the shared J_{H}4 window and ten for full-length J_{H}4 and N114 V_{H} reduced these proportions to 1.0%, 0.7% and 0.5%, respectively (Figure 4H). In a study using a common GFP substrate[@liu2018], no-editor libraries yielded at most one call in 20,000 reads. AID substitutions were more frequently located at WRC/GYW hotspots than APOBEC3A substitutions, both in Wt cells (56% versus 14%; p = 0.0040) and in UNG/MSH2-deficient cells (37% versus 20%; p = 9.0 × 10^{−4}). In the repair-deficient background, however, their transition shares were similar (78% versus 76%; p = 0.74), showing that transition enrichment alone could not distinguish the two enzymes. p The second engineered dataset, a CRISPR-X antibody-evolution study, diversified an antibody V_{H} and published the mutations it found in each selected cluster[@devilder2019]. Comparing the two lists position by position, SHMtrace recovered 150 of the 150 published mutations and called none the authors did not, across all 42 cluster entries (Figure S18B). Because the comparator is an independently curated list rather than another run of the same software, this is the sharpest available test of variant identity on this route. p Two further datasets tested transfer to another laboratory's switch-region libraries and to a fourth platform, and showed where a deposit limits what can be asked. Sμ libraries under PI3Kδ inhibition[@compagno2017] confound genotype with library layout: six of nine wild-type libraries are single-end whereas every *Aicda*^{−/−} library is paired, and the mates do not overlap, so only the twelve paired libraries reach the switch repeats where AID acts (Figure S19). Analysed over those repeats, the nine *Aicda*^{−/−} libraries returned one call between them against 81–172 per wild-type library (p = 0.0021), and the wild-type calls carried the AID signature: targeting index 1.52–1.67 and 31–35% C→T or G→A. The calibration therefore transfers to an independent laboratory, platform and perturbation. The drug comparison does not follow from this deposit, because only one wild-type replicate per treatment reaches the repeats. p PacBio Sμ–Sγ1 junctions[@vincendeau2022] read the donor, the join and the acceptor on one 6,126-bp molecule, so the junction module was applied to a class-switch phenotype it was not tuned for (Figure S20). Mean microhomology was 1.01 nt in wild type and unchanged in *Shld1*^{−/−} (1.02 nt; p = 0.92), but rose without XLF (1.69 nt; p = 0.0092) and in the double mutant (1.59 nt; p = 9.3 × 10^{−5}), where junctions with ≥4 nt of microhomology rose from 7% to 18% (p = 0.0053) and blunt junctions fell from 21% to 7% (p = 0.0028); *Shld1*^{−/−} junctions instead carried more inserted bases (48% versus 31%; p = 0.0074). This is the shift toward microhomology-mediated end joining expected when classical non-homologous end joining is impaired, with no parameter fitted to the dataset. Reads per unique junction vary several-fold between samples (1.1–23.3), so junction counts measure long-range PCR recovery rather than switching efficiency; junction shape, not calibrated calls, is compared, and re-aligning each acceptor segment on its own places 19–47% of unique junctions at more than one equally good position, so breakpoints within repeats remain approximate.

## DISCUSSION
Measuring somatic hypermutation has long meant choosing between reading molecules accurately and reading enough of them. This study shows that the choice is no longer necessary, provided the background is measured rather than assumed. SHMtrace takes whatever a platform delivers, reads or chromatograms, and replaces the two assumptions that make low-frequency calling from single reads unreliable for SHM. Instead of trusting quality scores, it measures the background in the AID-null libraries that SHM studies already include, at the level of substitution type, strand and base quality, and checks that calibration by holding out each control in turn. Instead of treating reads as replicates, it tests animals. Both replacements held: 81% of planted J_{H}4 mutations and 90% on N114 V_{H} were recovered at the false-discovery proportions the threshold targeted, while a quality-based caller either missed most true mutations or, made permissive, called as much background as the uncorrected rule, and pooling reads produced false positives in more than 90% of simulated experiments without a true difference. The gap this fills is specific, and it sits between two mature bodies of software. Repertoire toolkits identify and summarise mutations well: IgBLAST assigns germlines[@ye2013], Change-O and SHazaM quantify load[@gupta2015], SHMTool compares datasets[@maccarthy2009], and S5F models targeting[@yaari2013]. They begin, however, from mismatches already taken to be real, which is why counting every mismatch to the germline separated AID from empty-vector N114 libraries by 2-fold where SHMtrace separated them by 188-fold. General-purpose variant callers do the opposite: they decide what is real, but from error models fitted to data of a different kind. LoFreq reads base qualities[@wilm2012]; ClairS and DeepSomatic learn from tumour–normal pairs where the variant sits in a large share of reads[@zheng2026,@park2026]. Neither class measures what a particular SHM library does wrong. SHMtrace closes that circuit by making the AID-null control, already part of every well-designed SHM experiment, the source of the error model, and by carrying the measurement through to the animal-level comparison that the biology requires[@lazic2010]. The held-out controls then set the threshold empirically, in the spirit of target–decoy analysis in proteomics[@elias2007], rather than by assuming the distribution the p-values would need for a correction such as Benjamini–Hochberg[@benjamini1995]. Seen this way, comparator performance is a matter of operating range rather than quality. LoFreq was designed for deep short-read data with calibrated qualities; its base-alignment correction suppressed nearly all nanopore calls here, and disabling it still limited recovery of low-support variants. That is not a defect on its intended data: a per-base error model fitted without matched controls has no way to reach this assay's library-specific background. The measurements say why. Above Q40, control error stopped decreasing and varied between runs (1–9 × 10^{−5}; Figure S2), so the quality score runs out of information exactly where SHM calling needs it. ClairS and DeepSomatic recovered clean variants at higher allele fractions but few real SHM calls, and chemistry and model mismatches further qualify external-data comparisons. Clair3 and DeepVariant found none of the planted mutations, as expected of callers built to genotype a sample against a reference rather than to find a variant carried by a few reads. Geneious provided greater single-read recall on J_{H}4 and *Pim1* but more false calls on each amplicon. These are choices a study can make deliberately: sensitive mode raises the empirical target to 0.20 and recovers 72% of single-read mutations on J_{H}4 and 87% on N114 V_{H} at observed FDR 0.18 and 0.16, and events still unresolved individually are estimated in aggregate rather than discarded. Because the background is measured rather than modelled, the same engine reads all four platforms, and what each contributes becomes a question about the experiment rather than the software. A Sanger clone is one molecule read once, so its mutations are read directly; there is no read support to weigh against a background, and so nothing for a per-call test to work on, and the AID-null clones measure a background reported beside the counts, with how much of each animal's total it explains. Nanopore and Illumina libraries carry matched AID-null arms, so each candidate is tested against the errors those controls make. Nanopore spans the whole amplicon, which made the 3′ decline visible without tiling, identified clones from the V(D)J junction and ordered their mutations into trees; Illumina gives depth without that linkage. PacBio combines length with per-base accuracy, and reading the donor, the join and the acceptor on one molecule recovered a known end-joining phenotype from a deposit with no AID-null arm, using junction shape in place of calibrated calls. Figure S21 sets out what each measured. Agreement between platforms is itself evidence: nanopore and Sanger reads from the same six mice gave substitution spectra correlated at r = 0.92 and the same AID-null contrast, so the cloning-free route reports the biology the classical assay reports. Applying the same engine to other laboratories' data turned out to test it harder than any internal benchmark. Published sequence collections support reproducible reanalysis when clones are linked to animals and genotypes: SHMtrace recovered the principal J_{H}4 frequency and G:C comparisons, matched SW_{HEL} counts across five arms and twelve substitution types (2,804 of 2,809 tabulated mutations; r = 1.0000), and recovered 150 of 150 mutations an antibody-evolution study curated by hand, calling none its authors did not. Agreement was not complete, and the exceptions are as informative: Wt frequencies differed after denominator harmonisation, the published *Mkln1* A:T decrease was not significant in the reanalysis, and the SW_{HEL} total-mutation inference differed under a different test (Tables S4 and S5; Figures S14–S16). The Sanger re-analysis also surfaced bookkeeping problems, which SHMtrace now checks and reports rather than silently resolving. Deposits that include explicit clone-to-animal assignments and the original counting conventions, alongside sequences and chromatograms, would make such comparisons routine. What this enables in practice is a short experiment-to-answer path that keeps its own evidence: read accounting, fixed-seed sampling and one engine behind the desktop and command-line interfaces, with every exclusion, threshold and flagged site written out for inspection. The power analysis sets the honest expectation. How many animals a study needs depends on the difference it seeks: under variation estimated from three Wt J_{H}4 mice, the AID-competent against AID-null contrasts made here separate at three animals per group, whereas a two-fold difference between two AID-proficient groups would require approximately twenty, with considerable uncertainty in that estimate (Figure S8B). Sequencing depth cannot substitute for independent biological samples, and no analysis can recover a comparison the design did not support. Within that boundary the framework extends readily. It needs only a target, matched AID-null libraries and a sample sheet, which is why it transferred without retuning to off-target loci, switch regions, engineered diversification and base-editor substrates across twelve loci from eleven laboratories. Molecule-level consensus is the natural next addition: UMI collapsing of long reads already reduced technical error 57-fold[@karst2021], and combining it with control-calibrated calling should push the measurable range below what single reads can resolve.

## LIMITATIONS
SHMtrace relies on representative AID-null controls prepared and sequenced alongside the samples. Error profiles can vary between runs and basecalling configurations, and calibration using controls from a different run was not evaluated. With one nanopore run per assay, the present results do not establish performance across independent runs. The reported variants remain subject to technical uncertainty. Without UMIs, early PCR errors, recombination and repeated sequencing of PCR-derived molecules cannot be reliably distinguished from genuine mutations or expanded biological lineages. Mutation trees therefore describe relationships among reads rather than independently established cellular lineages. The default ≥50% allele-frequency filter can also remove genuine mutations shared by a dominant lineage. Excluded sites are reported for review, and experiments with a known starting sequence can use an optional reference policy that retains high-frequency differences. Single-read mutations that cannot be called individually are estimated only in aggregate, and unique-variant densities require comparison at matched sequencing depth. The empirical false-discovery estimate depends on the available controls and can exceed its nominal target when background differs between libraries. Noisy-control exclusion requires at least three controls, and its effect should be reviewed. Sample-specific artifacts absent from the controls may still pass the test; spectrum and clustering flags can identify unusual patterns but require interpretation. At weakly mutated targets such as Pim1, signal remained close to background. The core validation used three animals per group for J_{H}4 and one locus per cell system. Settings were developed using these libraries and applied to public datasets without dataset-specific retuning, although the noisy-control exclusion rule was introduced after inspection of the Ramos results. Evaluation on an independent sequencing run with new biological samples would provide a stronger test of generalizability. Indel validation comprised nanopore spike-ins and one Illumina biological phenotype, while UMI validation used rRNA rather than SHM data. These experiments do not establish performance on biological nanopore indels or UMI-tagged SHM libraries; molecule-level consensus remains preferable when available. Two Sanger studies originated from the authors’ laboratory, while the independent Sanger study provided sequences without chromatograms or AID-null controls. Together, these datasets evaluate distinct parts of the workflow, with different limits on the conclusions each can support.
